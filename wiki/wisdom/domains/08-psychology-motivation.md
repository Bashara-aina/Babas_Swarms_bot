# Domain 08 — Psychology & Motivation

Understanding what drives human behavior is essential for designing products, systems, and AI interactions that people actually want to use — not because they're manipulated, but because they satisfy real psychological needs.

---

## Frankl — Logotherapy

**Type**: Framework | **Year**: 1946 | **Source**: Viktor Frankl, *Man's Search for Meaning*

**Core Insight**: The primary human drive is not pleasure (Freud) or power (Adler) but meaning. People can endure almost any suffering if they can find meaning in it. When people know their 'why', the 'how' becomes bearable. Meaning is not discovered — it is created through a meaningful task, through love, or through the attitude taken toward unavoidable suffering.

**Why it matters**: Extrinsic rewards (money, status) produce fragile motivation that disappears when rewards are removed. Meaning-based motivation is self-sustaining and resilient to setbacks.

**LEGION RULE**: "When [[Bashara-aina]] expresses frustration or demotivation, apply the Frankl question: 'What is the meaning this situation can have for you?' Help find the 'why' before solving the 'how'. Don't rush to fix the problem — first help frame it as meaningful."

**Applied to Bashara**: When the [[projects/cekwajar-id]] project felt overwhelming due to regulatory complexity, reframing it as "thousands of Indonesian workers who will make better retirement decisions because this exists" creates a meaning anchor that makes the frustration bearable and the work sustainable.

**Example**: Frankl survived Nazi concentration camps by finding meaning — he imagined his wife, imagined giving lectures about what he'd learned, imagined the books he would write. Other prisoners who had "nothing to live for" died. Meaning was the mechanism of survival.

## Deci & Ryan — Self-Determination Theory

**Type**: Framework | **Year**: 2000 | **Source**: Edward Deci & Richard Ryan, *Self-Determination Theory* (ongoing research program)

**Core Insight**: Humans have three basic psychological needs: autonomy (self-direction, volition), competence (Mastery, effectance), and relatedness (connection, belonging). Intrinsic motivation — the drive to do something because it is interesting or enjoyable in itself — requires all three. Extrinsic motivation (doing something for external reward) can crowd out intrinsic motivation when external rewards are contingent.

**Why it matters**: This explains why gamification often fails — adding points and badges to a task that has no inherent interest actually decreases motivation once the points are removed. Effective motivation design satisfies the three needs directly, not through points.

**LEGION RULE**: "For any system that motivates behavior (a product, an AI agent, a team), ask: does this satisfy autonomy (does the user feel in control?), competence (does the user feel they're getting better at something?), and relatedness (does the user feel seen and understood?)? If any is missing, the motivation is fragile."

**Applied to Bashara**: [[projects/rumahlabuh]] booking system should make owners feel competent (their property is professionally managed, bookings are increasing), autonomous (they can adjust settings their way), and related (they feel connected to guests who love their property, not just transaction records).

**Example**: Studies of hospital cleaning staff found that those who saw their work as contributing to patient recovery (relatedness, meaning) were more satisfied and performed better than those who saw it as just task completion. The actual tasks were identical — the meaning framing changed the experience.

## Dweck — Growth Mindset

**Type**: Framework | **Year**: 2006 | **Source**: Carol Dweck, *Mindset: The New Psychology of Success*

**Core Insight**: People operate with one of two implicit theories of ability: fixed mindset ("intelligence is static, failure means I'm not good enough") or growth mindset ("intelligence can be developed, failure means I haven't learned yet"). These aren't just beliefs — they are computational frameworks that determine what attention is paid to, what feedback is received, and how effort is allocated.

**Why it matters**: The same failure event produces completely different downstream behavior depending on the mindset it activates. Fixed mindset → avoidant behavior → stagnation. Growth mindset → reflective analysis → improvement.

**LEGION RULE**: "When [[Bashara-aina]] experiences a failure or setback, reframe it explicitly as data for growth. Don't just solve the problem — narrate the learning. 'This approach didn't work because X — next time we will try Y because Z.' This is not motivational fluff; it is mindset engineering."

**Applied to Bashara**: The POPW model training deadlock was not a failure — it was feedback. The fixed-mindset reading: "I can't build working multi-task models." The growth-mindset reading: "gradient synchronization is the key variable I hadn't isolated yet; this failure produced the diagnostic information I needed."

**Example**: Students given identical difficult problems, but told "this is to assess your intelligence" (fixed) vs "this will help you develop new neural pathways" (growth) — the growth framing group persisted 3× longer and showed better problem-solving. The same difficulty, completely different computational engagement.
