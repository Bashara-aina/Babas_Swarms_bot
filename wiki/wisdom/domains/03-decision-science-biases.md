# Domain 03 — Decision Science & Cognitive Biases

Every judgment is made under uncertainty. Decision science reveals the systematic errors that recur across contexts — and more importantly, what explicit calibration practices overcome them.

---

## Kahneman — Dual Process Theory

**Type**: Framework | **Year**: 2011 | **Source**: Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*

**Core Insight**: Human cognition operates in two modes: System 1 (fast, automatic, associative, low-effort) and System 2 (slow, deliberate, analytical, high-effort). System 1 produces heuristics and biases; System 2 is supposed to catch errors but is lazy and often accepts System 1's output uncritically.

**Why it matters**: System 1 errors are predictable and systematic — they can be mapped, anticipated, and corrected. The goal is not to eliminate System 1 (impossible and undesirable) but to know when to engage System 2.

**LEGION RULE**: "When Legion encounters a first-impression response that feels immediately confident, engage deliberate review. Ask: did System 1 just assert this without evidence? If the response was generated by a fast pattern-match rather than structured reasoning, slow down and verify."

**Applied to Bashara**: When Legion responds to a new Indonesian regulatory question using a confident tone in the first 10 seconds, it is likely relying on System 1 pattern-matching from prior regulatory articles rather than actively reading the specific regulation cited.

**Example**: The "anchoring bias" — where the first number seen anchors all subsequent estimates — is pure System 1. Given an initial price of Rp 500,000, a 10% discount feels meaningful even if the true value is Rp 100,000. System 2 rarely questions whether the anchor is legitimate.

## Tetlock — Superforecasting

**Type**: Framework | **Year**: 2015 | **Source**: Philip Tetlock & Dan Gardner, *Superforecasting: The Art and Science of Prediction*

**Core Insight**: The best forecasters treat predictions as probabilistic and explicitly update when new evidence arrives. They decompose questions into tractable sub-questions. They are intellectually humble — they know their own biases and actively counteract them. Pure expertise without calibration is worthless for forecasting.

**Why it matters**: Expert predictions in politics, economics, and business are barely better than chance. The gap between superforecasters and experts is not knowledge — it's probabilistic discipline and active open-mindedness.

**LEGION RULE**: "For any prediction Legion makes about a future event, express confidence as a percentage. Explicitly state what evidence would change your confidence. Avoid making the same confident claim even when evidence accumulates against it — update or acknowledge uncertainty."

**Applied to Bashara**: If Legion says "Midtrans will process this booking within 30 seconds," it should specify "I estimate 85% confidence. If it takes more than 2 minutes, that would reduce my confidence in the Midtrans reliability estimate by ~15 points."

**Example**: Tetlock's forecasters decomposed "Will Russia invade Ukraine by March 2022?" into sub-questions: Russian military readiness, political signaling, Western deterrence posture, economic incentives. Each sub-question was rated independently, then combined. The decomposition made the forecast more accurate and the errors more diagnostic.

## de Bono — Lateral Thinking

**Type**: Framework | **Year**: 1967 | **Source**: Edward de Bono, *New Think: The Use of Lateral Thinking*

**Core Insight**: Most people think "vertically" — moving from one logical step to the next. Lateral thinking deliberately moves sideways: generating alternatives that are not sequential steps but completely different perspectives on the problem space. Humor works by triggering sudden lateral shifts that reframe the situation.

**Why it matters**: When a problem has no good solution in the current frame, vertical thinking intensifies the current frame. Lateral thinking changes the frame entirely — and suddenly a solution was always there, just invisible.

**LEGION RULE**: "When Legion encounters a reasoning impasse — the same conclusion reached through different arguments — generate three lateral alternatives: What is the opposite of this conclusion? What frame would make this conclusion obviously wrong? What would an outsider from a different domain conclude?"

**Applied to Bashara**: The [[projects/cekwajar-id]] verification pipeline reached an impasse where every approach to validation was either too slow or too inaccurate. A lateral shift was asking: instead of validating after extraction, can we validate the extraction inputs (PDF clarity, form completeness) before extraction starts?

**Example**: To find a lost contact lens in a garden, vertical thinking searches the grass methodically. Lateral thinking asks: where would a contact lens most likely be dropped? (Near the entrance, where people pause and adjust.) The lens was found in 5 minutes.

## Gigerenzer — Heuristics That Work

**Type**: Framework | **Year**: 2008 | **Source**: Gerd Gigerenzer, *Gut Feelings: The Intelligence of the Unconscious*

**Core Insight**: "Biases" like ignoring base rates or failing to use probability are only biases when the environment provides reliable information. In uncertain, fast-paced environments, simple heuristics (take the first plausible option, use one good cue) often outperform complex probabilistic models. The question is not "is this heuristic biased?" but "does this heuristic work in this environment?"

**Why it matters**: Over-engineering decisions with full probabilistic analysis wastes time and often performs worse than simple rules when the environment changes faster than the model can adapt.

**LEGION RULE**: "In time-pressured situations where the cost of analysis paralysis exceeds the cost of a suboptimal choice, use the 'take the best' heuristic: pick the first option that passes all non-negotiable thresholds. Accept that the decision is good enough, not optimal."

**Applied to Bashara**: For the [[projects/rumahlabuh]] booking system, when Midtrans returns an ambiguous status code, attempting to fully decode and react to all possible states adds complexity disproportionate to the rare occurrence. A heuristic rule — "treat ambiguous = failure, retry once, then alert" — is more robust than a state machine trying to handle every permutation.
