Between 2020 and early 2026 there are very few papers that **directly** compare FiLM-style feature modulation (including conditional batch/layer normalization and related hypernetwork variants) against Transformer-style cross-attention while holding most of the architecture and task fixed.

The most relevant controlled comparisons are:

- **Neural-field decoders for 2D semantic segmentation** (Gromniak et al., ICANN 2023): Cross-attention conditioning clearly outperforms FiLM and concatenation at similar parameter counts on Potsdam semantic segmentation; FiLM never beats cross-attention on accuracy in this setup.[1]
- **Conditional neural fields for MRI translation (CoNeS, 2024)**: A FiLM/shift-modulated neural field (with a hypernetwork) significantly outperforms a hybrid CNN+Transformer (ResViT) on multi-sequence MRI translation (PSNR/SSIM) and downstream tumor segmentation, with favorable parameter/computational efficiency; however, the backbones differ (neural field vs ResViT), so this is not a pure conditioning swap.[2]
- **FlexLoc (2024)**: For multi-view, multimodal localization, conditional 1D convolutions and conditional layer normalization (CLN; a FiLM-style modulation of normalization parameters) substantially beat a cross-attentionâ€“based pose-fusion baseline in the *same Transformer-based backbone*, while adding â‰¤0.2% parameters and â‰¤0.6% MACs.[3]
- **Attention Beats Concatenation for Conditioning Neural Fields (Rebain et al., 2022)**: Shows that transformer-style attention conditioning over high-dimensional latent codes systematically outperforms concatenation and hyper-network conditioning across several tasks (image auto-encoding, NeRF, light fields) under matched compute; FiLM is discussed as a restricted hypernetwork but not benchmarked directly.[4]

No work was found that **cleanly exposes a dataset-size flip point** where FiLM overtakes cross-attention or vice versa. Where both have been compared, cross-attention tends to win on accuracy once models can afford the extra compute, while FiLM-style modulation wins on parameter/MAC efficiency and can match or exceed attention-based methods in some domains when paired with suitable backbones.

## Key Controlled Comparison: Neural Fields for 2D Semantic Segmentation (Gromniak et al., 2023)

Gromniak et al. study neural-field decoders for 2D semantic segmentation of the ISPRS Potsdam aerial imagery dataset, using a fixed ResNet-34 CNN encoder and swapping only the **conditioning strategy of the decoder**.[1]

### Setup

- Task: 2D semantic segmentation on Potsdam (6 classes).
- Backbone encoder: ResNet-34 (ImageNet-pretrained), identical across all decoders.[1]
- Decoder: coordinate-based neural field; three conditioning types:
  - **Concat**: latent code concatenated to input coordinates with skip concatenations at hidden layers.
  - **FiLM**: conditional batch normalization/FiLM-style feature-wise affine modulation; latent code generates scale/shift per layer via an MLP.
  - **Cross-attention Transformer**: queries derived from point coordinates attend over the encoder feature volume treated as tokens (Scene Representation Transformerâ€“style cross-attention).[1]
- Conditional code sources:
  - Global (single vector per image), local (per-coordinate via bilinear interpolation in feature map), or global+local concatenation.[1]
- Metrics: mean IoU and F-score at two image sizes (256Ã—256 and 512Ã—512) plus decoder parameter count.[1]

- **Accuracy**: Cross-attention beats FiLM and concatenation by a clear margin at both resolutions.
  - At 256Ã—256, cross-attention improves IoU by about **+0.029** absolute over the best FiLM configuration (0.758 vs 0.729).[1]

  - The paper uses a single dataset (Potsdam) with fixed training splits and does **not** sweep dataset size or shots per class, so no dataset-size flip point is reported.[1]

### Takeaways from Gromniak et al.

- Under a controlled encoder and task, with decoder capacity roughly matched, **Transformer cross-attention conditioning is strictly better** than FiLM/CBN-style modulation for this 2D semantic segmentation problem.[1]
- FiLM is competitive with concatenation and sometimes slightly better (especially with local or global+local codes), but it never catches up to cross-attention in this setting.[1]
- This is the cleanest â€œFiLM vs cross-attention in same architectureâ€ comparison in 2020â€“2026, and it favors cross-attention on accuracy and parameter efficiency at this scale and dataset.

## Conditional Neural Fields with Shift Modulation vs Transformer Blocks (CoNeS, 2024)

CoNeS (Conditional neural fields with shift modulation for multi-sequence MRI translation) introduces a coordinate-based neural field decoder conditioned via **shift modulation**, a FiLM variant that modulates only the additive bias per neuron based on a hypernetwork, and compares it against several baselines including **ResViT**, a hybrid CNN+Transformer image-translation model.[2]

### Setup

- Task: 2D MRI sequence-to-sequence translation (e.g., synthesize T1ce from T1/T2/FLAIR) on BraTS 2018 and an in-house vestibular schwannoma dataset.[2]
- CoNeS generator: Coordinate-based MLP neural field; per-pixel latent codes generated by a CNN hypernetwork; conditioning via FiLM-style affine modulation, with experiments showing that **shift-only modulation** (no scaling) is parameter-efficient and effective.[2]
- Baselines: pix2pix (UNet+GAN), pGAN (ResNet+GAN with perceptual loss), ResViT (CNN+Transformer bottleneck), and ASAP-Net (neural-field GAN without shared MLP).[2]
- Metrics: PSNR, SSIM, plus downstream tumor segmentation quality (Dice, 95% Hausdorff distance).[2]

### Quantitative Comparisons (FiLM-like vs Transformer baseline)

Representative numbers from BraTS 2018 T1ce translation (predicting T1ce from other sequences):[2]

- **CoNeS (shift modulation)**: PSNR â‰ˆ **31.2 dB**, SSIM **0.951**.[2]
- pix2pix: 30.1 dB, 0.941.
- pGAN: 30.7 dB, 0.943.
- **ResViT (Transformer bottleneck)**: 29.2 dB, 0.935.
- ASAP-Net: 30.8 dB, 0.948.[2]

On the more challenging VS dataset (T1ce translation):[2]

- **CoNeS**: PSNR **21.9 dB**, SSIM 0.638.
- pix2pix: 21.1 dB, 0.602.
- pGAN: 21.6 dB, 0.635.
- **ResViT**: â‰ˆ21.0 dB, 0.575.
- ASAP-Net: clearly worse SSIM and spectrum fidelity.[2]

Downstream segmentation with synthesized images (BraTS, missing T1ce):[2]

- Using CoNeS-synthesized T1ce improved Dice for tumor subregions by **6â€“30 percentage points** over zero-imputation and 6â€“20 points over ResViT/pGAN in many cases, while also reducing Hausdorff distances.[2]

### Parameter and Compute Considerations

- The paper emphasizes that **shift modulation greatly reduces the number of parameters** compared to per-pixel hypernetworks while maintaining or improving performance; for example, using shifts alone achieves similar or better PSNR/SSIM with roughly half the hypernetwork parameters compared to full FiLM (scale+shift).[2]
- CoNeS and ASAP-Net avoid transposed convolutions and Transformer blocks, leading to better spectral fidelity and lower compute than ResViT for similar or better image quality; ResViT is described as â€œheavy and data-demandingâ€ for low-level tasks.[2]

### Caveats

- This is **not** a clean conditioning swap on a fixed backbone: CoNeS uses a coordinate-based MLP decoder with FiLM-like conditioning, whereas ResViT uses a CNN+Transformer backbone with self-attention.[2]
- Still, it is a strong real-world example where **FiLM-style conditioning (shift modulation) wins decisively over a Transformer-based competitor** on both accuracy and robustness for medical image translation.

## Conditional Layer Normalization vs Cross-Attention Pose Fusion (FlexLoc, 2024)

FlexLoc addresses **zero-shot sensor pose shift** in multimodal, multi-view localization using conditional neural networks and directly contrasts FiLM-style conditional normalization against a cross-attentionâ€“based pose-fusion baseline in the same approximate architecture.[3]

### Setup

- Task: 2D localization (x, y) of a remote-controlled car using distributed multimodal sensors (RGB, depth, mmWave radar, audio) across 22 distinct sensor-perspective configurations.[3]
- Backbone: Shared ResNet-18 and Transformer encoders for each modality; early fusion via a Transformer encoder; identical base architecture across variants.[3]
- Conditioning methods compared:
  1. **CondConv** (Conditional 1D Convolution): 1D conv weights conditioned on sensor pose via a small MLP, inserted after adapters.
  2. **CLN (Conditional Layer Normalization)**: replaces BatchNorm/LayerNorm scale and bias (Î³, Î²) with values derived from pose (a FiLM-style modulation, but usually a single scalar Î³/Î² per layer).[3]
  3. **Cross-attention pose fusion**: pose tokens fused with sensor features via cross-attention inside Transformer encoders (baseline inspired by camera-pose fusion in diffusion models).[3]
  4. Pose concatenation: pose encoded as a token concatenated with input tokens.[3]
  5. Unconditional early-fusion and late-fusion baselines.[3]

### Quantitative Results: Zero-shot Perspectives

On five **unseen sensor perspectives**, main comparison (average Euclidean distance error in cm):[3]

- **FlexLoc (CondConv)**: **14.28 cm** Â± 1.16 across views.[3]
- **FlexLoc-Light (CLN)**: **22.04 cm** Â± 4.32.[3]
- Unconditional early fusion: 26.86 cm Â± 4.68.
- Late-fusion baseline (hard-coded transforms): 27.53 cm Â± 1.87.[3]

Over a specialized Transformer-only backbone, comparing pose-fusion mechanisms:[3]

- CondConv and CLN achieve about **20% lower error** than pose-concatenation.
- **Cross-attention pose fusion yields â‰ˆ56 cm error**, substantially worse than CondConv/CLN and even worse than unconditional baselines.[3]

### Parameter and Compute Overhead

FlexLoc reports parameter and MAC overhead relative to the unconditional backbone:[3]

### Interpretation

- **Accuracy**: Both CondConv and CLN **strongly outperform** cross-attention pose fusion within the same Transformer-based backbone; cross-attention was the worst pose-fusion method tested for this task.[3]
- **Efficiency**: CLN is essentially free (slightly *reduces* parameters/MACs by reusing normalization slots), and CondConv adds <1% overhead in params and MACs while nearly halving error vs baselines.[3]
- **Data efficiency**: The model is trained on 13 viewpoints and tested zero-shot on 5 unseen viewpoints; all pose-conditioned methods significantly improve over unconditional baselines, but CLN/CondConv are far more effective than cross-attention on the same training data.[3]

This is a direct and fairly clean demonstration that **FiLM-style normalization conditioning (CLN) can be more data- and compute-efficient and more accurate than cross-attentionâ€“based conditioning** for certain structured tasks (here, sensor-pose fusion in localization).

## Attention vs Hypernet/FiLM-style Conditioning in Neural Fields (Rebain et al., 2022)

While not explicitly a â€œFiLM vs cross-attentionâ€ study, Rebain et al. analyze conditioning mechanisms for neural fields and provide strong evidence that **attention-based set-latent conditioning dominates concatenation and hyper-network conditioning at high latent dimensions**.[4]

### Setup

- Tasks: 2D image auto-encoding (Tiled MNIST, CelebA-HQ), 3D radiance fields (NeRF-style), 4D light fields, across several datasets (HUMBI, SRN Chairs/Cars).[4]
- Conditioning methods:
  - Concatenation: latent vector concatenated to coordinates at each layer.
  - Hyper-network: latent code drives a secondary network predicting decoder weights.
  - Attention-based set-latent: latent represented as a set of tokens; cross-attention layers take queries from encoded positions and keys/values from latent tokens.

FiLM (feature-wise linear modulation) is discussed as a constrained hyper-network but is **not** benchmarked as a separate baseline.[4]

### Selected Quantitative Results

**Tiled MNIST (PSNR, higher is better):**[4]

- At 256 unique digits (most complex variant), latent dim 512:
  - Concatenation: **16.8 dB**.
  - Hyper-network: **21.8 dB**.
  - **Attention**: **24.6 dB**.
- For simpler variants (1 or 16 digits), attention still dominates at larger latent dimensions, especially when complexity increases.[4]

**CelebA-HQ auto-encoding (PSNR):**[4]

- Latent dim 8192 (high-dimensional latent):
  - Concatenation: 24.5 dB.
  - Hyper-network: 23.6 dB.
  - **Attention**: **26.3 dB**.
- Decoder params at this setting: attention ~1.7 M vs concatenation 2.6 M and hyper-network 2.5 M.[4]

**NeRF-style novel view synthesis (PSNR on HUMBI, SRN Chairs/Cars):**[4]

- Across all three datasets and latent dims {512, 2048, 8192}, the **best configuration is always attention with the largest latent size**; e.g., on SRN Chairs at N=8192, attention achieves 25.0 dB vs 23.5 dB (hyper-network) and 20.9 dB (concat).[4]

### Takeaways

- With **matched compute budgets** (and sometimes even fewer parameters), attention-based conditioning on a set-latent consistently yields the best reconstruction quality across complex, high-entropy tasks.[4]
- Authors explicitly note that attention provides a principled way to exploit **very high-dimensional conditioning** efficiently, whereas concatenation/hypernetworks saturate or degrade as latent dimension grows.[4]
- Because FiLM is treated as a restricted hypernetwork variant, these results indirectly suggest that **cross-attention becomes increasingly advantageous as conditioning dimensionality and task complexity grow**, especially when compute is available.[4]

However, there is **no dataset-size sweep** here; the key independent variable is latent dimension (i.e., representation complexity), not number of training examples.[4]

## Conditional Batch Normalization in Few-Shot Multi-modal Meta-Learning (Armengol-EstapÃ© et al., 2024)

This work is mainly negative evidence: it uses **conditional batch normalization (CBN)** to modulate a few-shot classifier based on an auxiliary language network and finds that improvements are small, dataset-dependent, and largely attributable to extra compute/parameters rather than the conditioning mechanism itself.[5]

### Setup

- Task: few-shot classification (CUB-200-2011, mini-ImageNet) with an auxiliary network predicting language attributes/captions; a â€œbridgeâ€ MLP converts auxiliary embeddings into Î³, Î² parameters for batch-norm layers of a ProtoNet++ classifier (SimpAux).[5]
- Modulation: normalization conditioning, similar in spirit to FiLM/CBN but applied to batch norm.[5]
- Baseline: ProtoNet++ without conditioning.[5]

### Results

- On **CUB 5-shot**, SimpAux (CBN conditioning) improves accuracy from **88.5 Â± 0.5%** to **90.0 Â± 0.7%**.[5]
- On **mini-ImageNet 5-shot**, the baseline slightly *outperforms* the conditioned model (75.4 Â± 0.4% vs 74.9 Â± 0.1%).[5]
- An ablation with a bridge network but no auxiliary input shows that much of the gain on CUB is due to extra capacity rather than meaningful language conditioning.[5]

### Relevance

- This paper does **not** compare against cross-attention baselines, but its discussion section explicitly contrasts normalization-based conditioning with more complex approaches that use attention or memory banks, arguing that â€œnormalization conditioning is a more lightweight approach that is easier to learn in small data regimes due to the reduced complexity of the modulation factors.â€[5]
- It therefore supports the **qualitative** claim that CBN/FiLM-style mechanisms are attractive in low-data regimes, although in this particular implementation the gains are modest and not robust across datasets.[5]

## Conceptual Relations: FiLM, CBN, and Attention

The Distill article â€œFeature-wise transformationsâ€ provides helpful context on how FiLM-style conditioning differs fromâ€”and can complementâ€”attention mechanisms.[6]

### FiLM and CBN as Feature Modulation

- **FiLM**: applies a per-channel affine transformation to intermediate activations, with scale and shift predicted from a conditioning input (e.g., text question, style vector).[6]
- **Conditional Batch/Layer Normalization**: equivalent to applying FiLM *after* normalization, with Î³(cond), Î²(cond) replacing fixed affine parameters; de Vries et al. show that conditional BN can be viewed as a specific instance of FiLM.[6]
- Advantages:
  - Very low parameter and compute overhead (just two small vectors per layer per context).[6]
  - Naturally compatible with existing CNN/Transformer backbones.
  - Encourages parameter sharing and typically trains easily in low-data regimes.[6][5]

### Attention and Cross-Attention as Dynamic Routing

- Self-attention and cross-attention perform **content-based routing**: queries at each position attend to keys/values representing other positions or modalities.[4]
- They induce dynamic, input-dependent mixing of features, whereas FiLM/CBN primarily rescale and shift existing feature channels.[6][4]
- Advantages (empirically, from Rebain et al. and Gromniak et al.):[1][4]
  - Better exploitation of high-dimensional conditioning.
  - Improved ability to model long-range dependencies and high-entropy signals.
  - Superior accuracy under matched or even lower parameter budgets when compute allows quadratic (or near-quadratic) attention cost.

### Hybrid Patterns

Modern generative models (e.g., diffusion Transformers, DiTs) often use **both** mechanisms: cross-attention (or unified attention) plus FiLM-style adaptive layer normalization (e.g., AdaLN-Zero, FiLM-style LayerNorm conditioning).[7][8]

- Conditioning signals (timestep, class label, text embeddings) are embedded and then used to produce **layer-wise FiLM parameters** (Î³, Î²) that modulate each Transformer block, while cross-attention integrates text/image tokens.[8][7]
- Emergent summaries and tutorials emphasize that this combination gives better control and parameter efficiency than cross-attention alone.[7][8]

## When Does FiLM-style Conditioning Outperform Cross-Attention?

Direct, clean **FiLM vs cross-attention on the same backbone and dataset** is rare; the strongest evidence where FiLM-style approaches outperform or rival attention/cross-attention is:

1. **Sensor-pose fusion for multimodal localization (FlexLoc)**:
   - CLN (FiLM-style normalization) and CondConv dramatically outperform cross-attention pose fusion within the same Transformer backbone while adding â‰¤0.2% of parameters and MACs.[3]
   - Cross-attention pose fusion reaches â‰ˆ56 cm error, far worse than CondConvâ€™s â‰ˆ14 cm and CLNâ€™s â‰ˆ22 cm.[3]
   - Here, the conditioning signal (7D sensor pose) is low-dimensional and structured; FiLM-style conditioning is a far better match than full cross-attention.

2. **MRI sequence translation with neural fields (CoNeS)**:
   - Shift-modulated neural fields (FiLM variant) outperform a Transformer-based ResViT generator by **0.5â€“2.0 dB PSNR and 0.8â€“1.6 points of SSIM** on several MRI translation tasks, with better spectrum fidelity and downstream segmentation quality, at lower or comparable complexity.[2]
   - The structured, continuous nature of coordinate-based MLPs plus FiLM-like conditioning seems advantageous compared to convolutional+Transformer backbones for this class of problems.

3. **General low-data and lightweight contexts (conceptual, multiple sources)**:
   - Armengol-EstapÃ© et al. argue that normalization conditioning is â€œmore lightweight and easier to learn in small data regimesâ€ compared to mechanisms that require complex meta-attention or memory banks, although their concrete gains are modest.[5]
   - Tutorials on conditional normalization (including CBN, AdaIN, and FiLM) emphasize their success in conditional GANs, style transfer, and conditional generation where compute and data budgets are limited relative to full transformer stacks.[9][6]

In all of these cases, **FiLM-style conditioning wins or is competitive because**:

- The conditioning signal is relatively low-dimensional (sensor pose, style vector, MRI sequence indices), so the expressivity of full cross-attention is not necessary.
- Parameter/MAC budgets are tight, and FiLM/CBN/CLN introduce negligible overhead while still providing strong conditional adaptation.
- Backbones (coordinate-based MLPs, CNNs) are already strong feature extractors; the conditioning layer mainly needs to tilt the representation, not perform complex cross-modal routing.

There is **no paper that sweeps dataset size and shows FiLM overtaking cross-attention at some explicit training-set threshold**. Evidence for FiLMâ€™s low-data advantages is currently qualitative and indirect.[5]

## When Does Cross-Attention Outperform FiLM-style Conditioning?

From the controlled neural-field segmentation experiment and the broader conditioning literature, cross-attention tends to win when:

1. **The task requires rich spatial or cross-modal routing and high-entropy conditioning**:
   - In 2D semantic segmentation with neural-field decoders, cross-attention conditioning on the encoder feature volume yields **~3â€“4 IoU points** over the best FiLM variants at comparable or lower parameters.[1]
   - In high-dimensional latent conditioning for auto-encoders, NeRF, and light fields, attention-based set-latents consistently outperform concatenation and hyper-networks, especially at large latent dimensions (up to 8192); attention sometimes has **fewer parameters and higher PSNR** than alternatives.[4]

2. **Conditioning vector is very high-dimensional**:
   - Rebain et al. show that concatenation and hyper-networks saturate or degrade as latent dimension increases, while attention continues to improve, indicating a structural advantage in exploiting high-dimensional context.[4]
   - Since FiLM can be viewed as a constrained hypernetwork, similar limitations likely apply when trying to absorb very large conditioning vectors purely through FiLM without attention.

3. **Large compute budgets are available**:
   - Cross-attention introduces at least quadratic cost in token count; its benefits in tasks like NeRF/light fields and semantic segmentation were established under budgets of tens of thousands of GPU-hours.[4]
   - Where those budgets are acceptable, cross-attention appears to be a better asymptotic choice for modeling complex conditional distributions.

Again, **no work directly reports a training-set-size flip point**, but qualitatively:

- Cross-attention seems to dominate **as both dataset complexity and conditioning dimensionality grow**, provided compute is not a bottleneck.[1][4]
- FiLM/CBN/CLN are preferred in **low-dimensional conditioning and/or low-resource settings**.

## Open Gaps and Missing Data

The userâ€™s questions about **â€œAt what dataset size does the gap flip?â€** and **â€œWhen does FiLM outperform cross-attention?â€** cannot be precisely answered with current literature:

- None of the identified works sweep training-set size while holding architecture and conditioning type fixed.
- The only systematic sweeps are over **latent dimension** (Rebain et al.) and **image size / code source** (Gromniak et al.), not sample count.[1][4]
- Where FiLM-style methods outperform transformer-based ones (CoNeS, FlexLoc), multiple confounding factors change simultaneously (backbone class, loss functions, data domain), making it impossible to attribute gains solely to the conditioning type.[2][3]

A rigorous answer to these questions would require **new experiments** that:

1. Fix the backbone (e.g., ResNet or ViT), dataset, and task.
2. Implement parallel FiLM/CBN/CLN vs cross-attention conditioning variants with carefully matched parameter/MAC budgets.
3. Systematically sweep training-set size (e.g., 1%, 5%, 10%, 25%, 50%, 100%) and measure accuracy, calibration, and overfitting.

No such controlled study appears to have been published as of early 2026.

## Practical Guidelines for Architecture Choice

Based on the available evidence, the following **heuristic guidelines** are supported:

1. **Use FiLM/CBN/CLN (feature modulation) when**:
   - The conditioning signal is **low- to moderate-dimensional** (labels, styles, sensor poses, a small number of scalars or short embeddings).
   - You have **tight parameter/MAC budgets** or real-time constraints; CLN/CBN typically add â‰ˆ0% overhead.[5][3]
   - The task is low-level or structured (image-to-image translation, localization) where the main backbone already captures most structure and conditioning only needs to tilt activations.[2][3]
   - You operate in **small-data regimes** and want a simple, stable conditioning mechanism.[5]

2. **Use cross-attention (possibly with FiLM-style normalization) when**:
   - The conditioning signal is **high-dimensional** (long text prompts, long sequences, large latent codes) and you need rich, position-dependent fusion.[7][4]
   - Tasks involve **complex cross-modal routing** (e.g., general multimodal reasoning, 3D scene understanding with many views) where attentionâ€™s content-based addressing shines.[4][1]
   - Compute is not the primary bottleneck and you can afford quadratic or near-quadratic costs.
   - You already use Transformer blocks and can easily integrate AdaLN/FiLM-style LN modulation on top.[8][7]

3. **Hybrid strategies are often best**:
   - Many state-of-the-art DiTs and multimodal transformers use **both** cross-attention and FiLM-style normalization (AdaLN-Zero, FiLM-LN): attention for routing, FiLM for efficient global conditioning.[8][7]
   - FlexLoc shows that combining CondConv and CLN can improve over CLN alone, though not over CondConv, suggesting some redundancy but also complementarity.[3]

In summary, **unified, general statements like â€œFiLM beats cross-attention below N samplesâ€ are not yet supported by published empirical evidence**. What can be said is:

- For complex, high-entropy conditioning with ample compute, **cross-attention is usually the winning choice**.[1][4]
- For structured, low-dimensional conditioning under tight resource constraints, **FiLM/CBN/CLN can outperform or rival cross-attention**, as demonstrated in FlexLoc and CoNeS.[2][3]

Tradeoffs: FiLM vs crossâ€‘attention in practice
Expressivity vs locality
FiLM:
Per-channel scaling/shifting is global over spatial locations (and often over time), unless applied at multiple layers of a feature hierarchy.
It cannot, by itself, choose where in the feature map to emphasize or suppress context; all spatial positions in a channel are scaled equally.
However, if your backbone (e.g., FPN) already encodes spatial structure and multi-scale context, global per-channel modulation is often sufficient for many tasks.
Crossâ€‘attention:
Models full pairwise interactions between query tokens (e.g., joints, text tokens) and key/value tokens (e.g., spatial patches, object features).
Naturally captures spatially varying influence: a specific joint can attend to particular image regions, enabling localized conditioning that FiLM alone cannot directly express.
