<section id="overview">
<h2><span class="sec-num">01</span> Overview</h2>
<p class="subtitle">What is POPW? Problem statement, dataset facts, and why this matters</p>

<div class="card card-accent">
<h4 style="margin-top:0">Methodology: where do the 51D pose values come from?</h4>
<p style="font-size:0.88rem;margin-bottom:8px">They are <strong>not</strong> from an external OpenPose/HRNet/ViTPose API in the POPW training loop. The same network predicts <strong>17 joint heatmaps</strong> from FPN features, then <strong>soft-argmax</strong> yields differentiable $(x,y)$, and per-joint <strong>visibility/confidence</strong> scalars come from heatmap peaks (computed under <code>no_grad</code> / <code>.detach</code> for FiLM conditioning). So the 51D vector is <strong>on-manifold with the trained model</strong> &mdash; comparable in spirit to HRNet-style heatmaps but end-to-end with POPW.</p>
<p style="font-size:0.85rem;color:var(--text-muted);margin-bottom:10px"><strong>Ground-truth pose files</strong> in the released IKEA ASM pipeline are produced with an <strong>OpenPose-style 19-keypoint</strong> export mapped to <strong>COCO-17</strong> in the dataloader (<code>openpose19_to_coco17</code>). That is independent of the <strong>in-network</strong> heatmap &rarr; soft-argmax path above, which is what training optimizes end-to-end.</p>

<h4 style="margin-top:0">Data augmentation (<code>IKEAMultiTaskDataset._apply_augmentation</code>)</h4>
<p style="font-size:0.88rem;margin-bottom:8px">Applied only when <code>augment=True</code> (training). After resize to <code>(img_w, img_h) = (640, 480)</code> from <code>C.IMG_SIZE</code> (tensor then <code>[3, H, W] = [3, 480, 640]</code>):</p>
<ul style="font-size:0.85rem;line-height:1.6;margin:0;padding-left:1.2rem;color:var(--text-muted)">
<li><strong>Horizontal flip</strong> &mdash; 50% chance; bbox and keypoints mirrored; COCO flip pairs swapped.</li>
<li><strong>Global brightness scale</strong> &mdash; 50% chance; multiply RGB by <span class="mono">Uniform(0.7, 1.3)</span>, clip to [0,255].</li>
<li><strong>Contrast about mean</strong> &mdash; 50% chance; <span class="mono">(img − mean) × Uniform(0.7, 1.3) + mean</span>, clipped.</li>
<li><strong>Saturation-style scaling</strong> &mdash; 30% chance; mix toward grayscale then scale deviation, clipped.</li>
<li><strong>Gaussian noise</strong> &mdash; 20% chance; <span class="mono">𝒩(0, 5)</span> per pixel, clipped.</li>
</ul>

<p>Assembly action recognition on IKEA ASM is among the most challenging fine-grained activity understanding tasks in computer vision. The dataset contains 33 distinct assembly actions &mdash; many of which are visually near-identical. Consider "insert dowel" versus "tighten bolt": both involve a person reaching toward a furniture surface with a small part in hand. From a purely visual perspective (RGB pixels alone), the difference between these two actions can be as subtle as a 10-degree change in wrist angle or a slight shift in finger configuration.</p>
<p>Existing single-task approaches treat action recognition as an isolated classification problem, ignoring the rich geometric information encoded in human body pose. A person's skeleton geometry &mdash; the angles of their elbows, the position of their wrists relative to the assembly surface, whether they are bending or standing upright &mdash; provides powerful discriminative signals that complement raw visual features. POPW's key insight is that this geometric information should not merely be concatenated with visual features, but should <em>condition</em> the visual feature extraction itself, telling the network which visual patterns to amplify or suppress given the current body configuration.</p>

<section id="pose-head">
<h2><span class="sec-num">03</span> Pose Heatmap Head</h2>
<p class="subtitle">From FPN features to 17 joint heatmaps, soft-argmax, and the 51D pose vector</p>

<div class="card">
<h4>Forward path</h4>
<p>FPN features are decoded with transposed convolutions to a heatmap tensor <code>[B, 17, 64, 85]</code> &mdash; one channel per COCO joint at roughly 1/8 resolution relative to the input width/height layout used in POPW. Each channel is a soft 2D distribution over joint location.</p>
<p><strong>Soft-argmax</strong> extracts differentiable $(x,y)$ per joint from each heatmap (expected value under the normalized softmax). Gradients flow into the heatmap head and thus the backbone for keypoint positions.</p>
<p><strong>Confidences</strong> are taken as the max response per heatmap (or equivalent peak score). Those 17 scalars are detached (<code>torch.no_grad</code>) before concatenation into <code>pose_flat</code> so they act as non-differentiable gating features for FiLM rather than a second path that would shortcut pose supervision.</p>
</div>

<h4>PCKh@0.5 Metric Definition</h4>
$$\text{PCKh@0.5} = \frac{\#\text{keypoints where } \|p_i - g_i\|_2 \leq 0.5 \times \text{head\_size}}{\#\text{total keypoints}}$$
<p>where $\text{head\_size} = \|p_{\text{head\_top}} - p_{\text{neck}}\|_2$ is the Euclidean distance between the top of the head and the neck joint in ground truth. This normalizes the distance threshold by body scale, making the metric robust across subjects at different distances from the camera.</p>
<p>Our <code>compare_results.json</code> eval uses normalized coordinates: improved4 achieves <strong>99.53%</strong> at PCK@0.05 and <strong>99.89%</strong> at PCK@0.01; improved4_film is <strong>99.58%</strong> / <strong>99.89%</strong> at the same thresholds.</p>
</div>

<div class="definition-box" style="border-color:var(--purple);">
<h4 style="color:var(--purple);">Why BCE, not softmax Cross-Entropy for joint confidence?</h4>
<p>Each joint's confidence is an <strong>independent binary question</strong>: "Is this joint visible/detected?" This is modeled as $p(c_j = 1)$ per joint $j$, trained with Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE):</p>
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{conf}} = -\frac{1}{17}\sum_{j=1}^{17}\left[y_j \log(\hat{c}_j) + (1-y_j)\log(1-\hat{c}_j)\right]$$
<p>Softmax CE would force competition between joints &mdash; detecting one joint would suppress another since softmax outputs sum to 1. BCE treats each joint as an i.i.d. Bernoulli trial: detecting the left wrist has zero effect on the right ankle's confidence. This is both physically correct (joint visibility is independent) and mathematically necessary (we want all 17 confidences to be simultaneously high when all joints are visible).</p>
</div>

<h3>4b. Why Exactly 2048 &gamma; and &beta; Values?</h3>
<div class="card">
<p>ResNet-50's C5 (the final convolutional stage) outputs <code>[B, 2048, 20, 15]</code> at our 640&times;480 input. The 2048 channel count comes from ResNet-50's bottleneck design: the final expansion layer has 4&times; the base width (512 &times; 4 = 2048). FiLM operates <strong>channel-wise</strong> &mdash; each $\gamma_i$ and $\beta_i$ scales/shifts exactly one channel &mdash; so we need exactly <strong>2048 each</strong>.</p>
</div>

<h3>4c. Activation Functions: Why 1+tanh for &gamma;, Unbounded for &beta;</h3>
<p><strong>For &gamma; (scale):</strong> $\gamma_i = 1 + \tanh(\text{raw}_i) \in (0, 2)$</p>
<p>Four purposes: (1) <strong>Identity initialization</strong> &mdash; at init when raw &asymp; 0, &gamma; = 1.0. (2) <strong>Bounded amplification</strong> &mdash; max 2.0&times;. (3) <strong>Bounded suppression</strong> &mdash; approaches but never reaches 0. (4) <strong>No polarity inversion</strong> &mdash; &gamma; &gt; 0 always.</p>
<p><strong>For &beta; (shift):</strong> $\beta_i = \text{raw}_i$ &mdash; unbounded linear output. No risk of sign-flip instability.</p>
<p style="font-size:0.88rem;color:var(--text-muted)"><strong>Why not $2\sigma(\cdot)$ for &gamma;?</strong> A sigmoid maps to $(0,1)$; doubling gives $(0,2)$ with identity near $\sigma=0.5$. That only <em>suppresses</em> toward 0 or <em>amplifies</em> toward 2 from a 1.0 baseline in a fixed sigmoid shape. <strong>$1+\tanh$</strong> is symmetric around 0 in the latent: negative logits smoothly suppress below 1, positive logits amplify above 1, with bounded derivatives. Either is valid; we follow the interactive explorer above for intuition.</p>
<p style="font-size:0.88rem;color:var(--text-muted)"><strong>Hidden layers: ReLU vs tanh for &beta; MLP?</strong> The <em>output</em> &beta; is linear. Internally we use ReLU for capacity; if the <em>last</em> nonlinearity on &beta; were ReLU-only, the <em>bias</em> path from negative pre-activations would be clipped to 0 &mdash; asymmetric. Keeping a final <strong>linear</strong> head on &beta; avoids forcing all shifts nonnegative.</p>

<h3>6a. Four Loss Functions Compared</h3>

<p><strong>L2 Loss (MSE):</strong> $\mathcal{L}_{L2}(x) = x^2$ &mdash; Gradient $2x$ grows without bound. Outliers dominate training.</p>

<p><strong>L1 Loss (MAE):</strong> $\mathcal{L}_{L1}(x) = |x|$ &mdash; Gradient $\pm 1$ everywhere. Small errors get no extra signal.</p>

<p><strong>Huber Loss:</strong></p>
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{Huber}}(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}x^2 & \text{if } |x| < \delta \\ \delta(|x| - \frac{\delta}{2}) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
<p>Fixed global &delta; cannot be optimal for all joints simultaneously.</p>

<p><strong>Wing Loss (ours):</strong></p>
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{Wing}}(x) = \begin{cases} w \cdot \ln\left(1 + \frac{|x|}{\epsilon}\right) & \text{if } |x| < w \\ |x| - C & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
<p>where $C = w - w \cdot \ln\left(1 + \frac{w}{\epsilon}\right)$ ensures continuity at $|x| = w$.</p>

<div class="card card-accent">
<h4 style="margin-top:0">Deriving $C$ at the boundary $|x| = w$</h4>
<p style="font-size:0.88rem">For $x &gt; 0$, the log branch gives $L_{\log}(w) = w\ln(1 + w/\epsilon)$. The linear branch is $L_{\mathrm{lin}}(x) = x - C$. Require <strong>equal values</strong> at $x = w$: $w\ln(1 + w/\epsilon) = w - C$, hence $C = w - w\ln(1 + w/\epsilon)$.</p>
<p style="font-size:0.88rem;margin-bottom:0">Require <strong>equal derivatives</strong> (smooth join): left derivative at $w$ is $\frac{d}{dx}\big[w\ln(1+x/\epsilon)\big]_{x=w} = \frac{w}{\epsilon + w}$; right derivative is $1$. These are <em>not</em> equal in general — Wing uses a <strong>continuous</strong> (C⁰) join; the kink still yields subgradient behavior suitable for optimization. The constant $C$ removes the <em>jump</em> in loss values that would occur if the linear tail were $x$ without offset.</p>
</div>

<h3>6b. Our Parameters</h3>
<div class="table-wrap">

<h3>6c. Gradient Analysis &mdash; The Key Insight</h3>
$$\frac{d\mathcal{L}_{\text{Wing}}}{dx} = \begin{cases} \frac{w}{\epsilon + |x|} & \text{if } |x| < w \\ \text{sign}(x) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

<p>Wing loss provides <strong>higher gradient for small errors</strong> (driving precision) and <strong>equal gradient for large errors</strong> (matching L1's outlier resistance). This is exactly what precision-critical keypoint tasks need.</p>

<h3>6g. Professor Questions</h3>
<div class="accordion">
  <div class="accordion-item">
    <button class="accordion-trigger" onclick="this.parentElement.classList.toggle('open')">Q: "Why not just use Huber loss?"</button>
    <div class="accordion-body">
      <p>Huber has a fixed global &delta; threshold and provides <strong>quadratic gradient</strong> for small errors &mdash; meaning gradient <em>decreases</em> toward zero as error shrinks. Wing's log region provides <strong>stronger gradient for small errors</strong> than Huber's quadratic region.</p>
      <p>Concrete comparison at $|x| = 0.001$ with &delta; = 0.01:</p>
      <p>&bull; <strong>Huber gradient:</strong> $|x| = 0.001$ (inside quadratic region, gradient = $x = 0.001$)</p>
      <p>&bull; <strong>Wing gradient:</strong> $w/(\epsilon + |x|) = 0.05/(0.005 + 0.001) = \mathbf{8.33}$</p>
      <p>Wing delivers <strong>8,330&times; stronger gradient</strong> at subpixel errors. Wing is specifically designed for keypoint precision where the last fraction of a pixel matters for pose accuracy feeding into FiLM.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="accordion-item">
    <button class="accordion-trigger" onclick="this.parentElement.classList.toggle('open')">Q: "How was w=0.05 chosen? Why not w=10?"</button>
    <div class="accordion-body">
      <p>The value $w = 0.05$ is in <strong>normalized coordinates</strong> (keypoint positions are normalized to [0, 1]). In pixel space at 640px width, this equals $0.05 \times 640 \approx 32\text{px}$.</p>
      <p>This threshold separates two regimes:</p>
      <p>&bull; <strong>Precision zone</strong> ($|x| < 0.05$): subpixel corrections get log-amplified gradient. These are the "almost right" joints that need to be pushed to exact positions for accurate FiLM conditioning.</p>
      <p>&bull; <strong>Outlier zone</strong> ($|x| \geq 0.05$): linear L1-like gradient prevents outlier keypoints (occluded, mis-detected) from dominating training with quadratic explosion.</p>
      <p>The value was selected based on the natural precision boundary for body keypoints at our 640&times;480 resolution &mdash; 32px is approximately the width of a wrist or ankle in a standing person at typical IKEA ASM camera distance. Errors smaller than a joint size need precision refinement; errors larger than a joint size are likely detection failures that should be treated as outliers.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<h3>7b. Three-Stage Equation Build-Up</h3>
<p style="font-size:0.82rem;color:var(--text-muted)">Staged reveal (professor pacing):</p>
<div style="display:flex;flex-wrap:wrap;gap:8px;margin:8px 0 16px">
<button type="button" class="hdr-btn" onclick="showCbStage(1)">Show CE Problem</button>
<button type="button" class="hdr-btn" onclick="showCbStage(2)">Add CB Weighting</button>
<button type="button" class="hdr-btn" onclick="showCbStage(3)">Add Focal Term</button>
</div>

<div id="formal-problem-block">
<div id="cbStageBlock1">
<h4>Stage 1 — Standard Cross-Entropy</h4>
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}(p, y) = -\log(p_y)$$
<p>Treats every sample equally regardless of class frequency. A model that always predicts the most common class can achieve deceptively high accuracy.</p>
<div class="card card-danger" style="padding:10px;margin-top:8px;">
  <strong style="color:var(--red)">CE failure mode:</strong>
  <span style="font-size:0.85rem;">Always predicting <code>Lay (frame)</code> yields 520/3400 = <strong>15.3%</strong> accuracy without learning assembly semantics.</span>
</div>
</div>

<div id="cbStageBlock2" style="display:none">
<h4>Stage 2 — Add Class-Balanced Weighting</h4>
$$\alpha_c = \frac{1 - \beta_{\text{CB}}}{1 - \beta_{\text{CB}}^{n_c}}$$
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CB}}(p, y) = -\alpha_y \log(p_y)$$

<div class="table-wrap">
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Symbol</th><th>Value</th><th>Meaning</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>$\beta_{\text{CB}}$</td><td><code>0.9999</code></td><td>Controls weight decrease rate as class size grows. β→1: inverse-frequency. β→0: no weighting. β=0.9999 gives "effective number of samples" weighting.</td></tr>
<tr><td>$n_c$</td><td>varies</td><td>Training sample count for class $c$</td></tr>
<tr><td>$\alpha_c$</td><td>computed</td><td>Re-weighting factor — higher for rare classes, lower for common classes</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>
</div>

<div id="cbStageBlock3" style="display:none">
<h4>Stage 3 — Add Focal Down-weighting</h4>
$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{CB-Focal}}(p, y) = -\alpha_y (1 - p_y)^{\gamma_{\text{focal}}} \log(p_y)$$

<p>When the model is 95% confident on a sample ($p_y = 0.95$): focal factor = $(0.05)^2 = 0.0025$. That sample's loss is reduced 400×, freeing gradient capacity for hard, uncertain samples.</p>

<div class="table-wrap">
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Symbol</th><th>Value</th><th>Meaning</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>$(1-p_y)^{\gamma_{\text{focal}}}$</td><td>computed</td><td>Down-weighting factor for easy samples</td></tr>
<tr><td>$\gamma_{\text{focal}}$</td><td><code>2.0</code></td><td>Focal exponent. γ=0: standard CE. γ=2: standard focal from Lin et al. (2017)</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>
</div>
</div>

<h4 style="margin-top:20px">Focal factor $(1-p)^\gamma$ for different $\gamma$ (at fixed $p$)</h4>
<p style="font-size:0.8rem;color:var(--text-muted)">Larger $\gamma$ more aggressively down-weights easy examples (high $p_y$). $\gamma=2$ is the RetinaNet default; $\gamma=0$ recovers no focal modulation.</p>
