Published Ablation — Original Wing Loss (Facial Landmarks)
Feng et al. tested multiple w/ε combinations, reported in their Table 2 on the AFLW dataset using Normalised Mean Error (NME) as the metric — not PCKh. Their key findings:

CNN-6 (64×64 input, single-stage): optimal at w=10, ε=2

CNN-7 (128×128 input, two-stage refinement): optimal at w=15, ε=3

They explicitly tested w = {5, 10, 15} with matching ε values; w=10/ε=2 was the best for a single-stage model while w=15/ε=3 yielded slight further gains in the second-stage fine-grained network

The scaling pattern is informative: larger w is preferred when input resolution increases, because the pixel-level error magnitude grows proportionally and the linear regime needs to cover a wider range.

Why No PCKh Ablation for Body Keypoints?
Human pose estimation keypoints
Human pose estimation keypoints
This is the critical gap in the literature. There are two reasons:

Wing Loss breaks on heatmap regression. The vast majority of competitive body pose estimation methods (for PCKh on MPII) use heatmap regression with MSE, not direct coordinate regression. The Adaptive Wing Loss paper explicitly warns that the original Wing Loss "is not applicable for heatmap regression" because small background-pixel errors accumulate excessive gradients and cause training to diverge. No published work has therefore performed a w/ε sweep on PCKh via the original Wing Loss.

Limited body-pose coordinate regression work. The few papers that do use coordinate regression for body joints (e.g., early cascaded regression) typically tune loss hyperparameters via grid search and do not publish a systematic sweep. The Adaptive Wing Loss paper validated its loss on the LSP body pose dataset (Table 8 in their paper), showing 4HG+AW achieves PCK@0.2 = 85.9% vs. 4HG+MSE = 81.8%, but no w/ε breakdown was reported for that body task.

Transformer vs CNN for small dataset action recognition in industrial assembly. ViT requires large pre-training data. For fine-tuning on 5000-10000 samples, does ResNet-50 outperform ViT-B/16? What is the crossover point in dataset size where transformers start to outperform CNNs?
Completed 2 steps
This is one of the most practically important questions in applied CV for industrial settings. The answer depends critically on whether you're training from scratch or fine-tuning, and the evidence from multiple controlled studies is clear.

The Core Asymmetry
ViT's weakness on small datasets is architectural, not accidental. CNNs have translation invariance and local feature locality baked into their convolutions — ViT must learn these properties from data. This means ViT is far more data-hungry when operating without large-scale pretraining. The original Dosovitskiy et al. ViT paper confirmed ViT-B/16 underperforms ResNets on ImageNet-1k (~1.3M images) but surpasses them on JFT-300M (~300M images).

Training from Scratch: ResNet-50 Dominates
The NeurIPS 2021 paper "Efficient Training of Visual Transformers with Small Datasets" (Liu et al.) provides the most controlled apples-to-apples comparison, training ResNet-50 and multiple ViT variants (~25M params each) from scratch:

Dataset (size) ResNet-50 CvT-13 (best ViT) Swin-T T2T-ViT-14
CIFAR-10 (50k) 91.78% 89.02% 59.47% 84.19%
CIFAR-100 (50k) 72.80% 73.50% 53.28% 65.16%
Flowers102 (2k) 46.92% 54.29% 34.51% 31.73%
SVHN (73k) 96.45% 91.47% 71.60% 95.36%
On your 5,000–10,000 sample regime, the gap is even more extreme — Swin-T without pretraining collapses to ~35–40% accuracy on small datasets while ResNet-50 stays in the 70–80% range. The root cause confirmed by visualization studies: ViT attention maps trained on small datasets learn random texture statistics rather than object-meaningful regions, while ResNet feature maps immediately lock onto object structure.

Fine-Tuning: The Crossover Is About Pretraining, Not Fine-Tuning Data
This is the key insight that changes everything for your scenario. Steiner et al. (Google Brain, TMLR 2022) showed that with ImageNet-21k pretraining, ViT-B/16 fine-tuned on even tiny datasets matches or beats ResNet-50:

Fine-tuning Dataset ResNet-50 Swin-T T2T-ViT-14
CIFAR-10 (~50k) 97.65% 97.95% 98.37%
CIFAR-100 (~50k) 85.44% 88.22% 87.33%
Flowers102 (~2k train) 96.59% 98.03% 97.98%
DomainNet Infograph (36k) 44.30% 41.07% 38.53%
The critical finding: for Pet37 (only ~3,300 fine-tuning images), no amount of from-scratch training can match fine-tuned ViT accuracy regardless of compute spent. Fine-tuned ViT-B/16 (IN-21k) on 5k–10k samples will be competitive with or superior to ResNet-50, while ViT trained from scratch on those samples is unusable.

Industrial Assembly-Specific Considerations
For your IKEA ASM / industrial assembly action recognition context, three factors shift the balance:

Domain shift: Industrial assembly footage is visually very different from ImageNet. The Steiner et al. study showed that on high-domain-shift datasets (DomainNet Infograph, satellite imagery), ResNet-50 fine-tuning beats ViT fine-tuning even with IN-21k pretraining. Assembly-line overhead RGB video is a medium-shift domain.

Inference speed: ResNet-50 achieves 45ms vs ViT-B 144ms on V100 (3.2× faster), with 40% less VRAM. For real-time assembly monitoring this matters.

Hybrid architectures: CvT-13 consistently matched ResNet-50 accuracy even when trained from scratch on small datasets, and ConvNeXt-Tiny (hybrid) achieves the best accuracy-latency tradeoff for industrial defect detection at 32ms.

Practical recommendation: For 5k–10k samples, use ResNet-50 if training from scratch or if inference speed is critical. Use ViT-B/16 pretrained on ImageNet-21k if you can afford the memory and want maximum accuracy. The strongest pragmatic option for your setting is a hybrid like CvT or ConvNeXt, which gets within 1–2% of ViT accuracy while being far more robust in the data-scarce regime.

# Assembly action recognition state of the art on IKEA ASM dataset 2021-2025.

What methods achieve the highest activity recognition accuracy?
Compare: RGB only vs RGB+pose vs multi-modal approaches.
What is the baseline accuracy and SOTA accuracy on IKEA ASM 16-class activity recognition?

Here is a comprehensive synthesis of the state-of-the-art on the IKEA ASM dataset for assembly action recognition, covering RGB-only, RGB+pose, and multi-modal approaches from 2021 through 2025–2026.

***

## IKEA ASM Dataset Overview

The IKEA ASM dataset is a ~3 million frame, multi-view, multi-modal furniture assembly video dataset featuring RGB, depth, and 3D skeleton data from three camera perspectives (front, side, top). It involves 48 participants and 371 unique assembly processes, with ~33 fine-grained atomic action classes. Evaluation is typically reported as **frame-wise accuracy (FA)**, **macro-recall**, and **mAP**, with FA being the primary metric.[^1_1][^1_2][^1_3]

***

## Original Baselines (WACV 2021)

The original benchmark paper tested classic 3D CNN architectures:[^1_2]


| Method | Modality | Frame-wise Accuracy (approx.) |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| C3D | RGB | ~25% |
| P3D ResNet | RGB | ~28% |
| I3D | RGB | ~30–34% |
| Frame-wise ResNet | RGB (per frame) | ~33–36% |
| I3D + Pose (multi-view) | RGB + Pose | Higher than RGB-only |
| Multi-view + Pose fusion | RGB + Depth + Pose | Best original SOTA |

The original paper explicitly notes that "combining views and pose gives an additional performance increase," while combining views + depth + pose yielded a slight disadvantage due to the inferior performance of depth-only methods at the time.[^1_4]

***

## RGB-Only: Foundation Models (2024)

A 2024 systematic study of vision foundation models on IKEA-ASM reveals that image-based models with advanced temporal fusion dramatically outperform earlier 3D CNNs:[^1_3]


| Foundation Model | Type | Top-1 Acc (trained view) | Mean Class Acc |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| VideoMAE | Video | 30.97% | 5.31% |
| CLIP (avg pooling) | Image | 26.47% | 6.39% |
| DinoV2 (avg pooling) | Image | 22.36% | 12.27% |
| X-CLIP | Video | **37.57%** | **20.87%** |
| CLIP + Self-Attn (all tokens) | Image | 45.19% | 22.61% |
| **DinoV2 + Self-Attn (all tokens)** | Image | **56.52%** | **25.58%** |

DinoV2 with self-attention-based temporal fusion outperforms all video foundation models on IKEA-ASM by ~4.7% in mean accuracy, demonstrating that image-based models with the right temporal aggregation can surpass video models.[^1_3]

***

## RGB + Pose vs. Multi-Modal Approaches

### Multi-View Fusion (2022)

A PLOS ONE study showed that multi-view feature fusion improves top-1 accuracy by **18.1%** over the single-view baseline on IKEA ASM, surpassing prior SOTA on the compositional action recognition setup. A ResNet50 baseline achieved 29.4% top-1, 55.1% top-3, 22.1% macro-recall, and 11.9% mAP.[^1_5][^1_6]

### RGB + Depth + Action History (2025–2026 SOTA)

The most recent single-frame anticipation framework (AAG+) achieves **72.38% top-1 recognition accuracy** on IKEA-ASM using only an RGB frame + learned action history context, rising to **72.95%** when depth is added. This outperforms dense video-based RGB-only methods:[^1_7]


| Method | Modalities | Input Frames | IKEA-ASM Top-1 Acc (Anticipation) |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| RULSTM | RGB | 14 | 26.37% |
| TempAgg | RGB | 37 | 26.90% |
| AVT | RGB | 10 | 27.10% |
| VLMAH | RGB + Action History | 8 | **52.31%** |
| AAG (single frame) | RGB-D + Action History | 1 | 44.66% |
| **AAG+** | **RGB-D + Action History** | **1** | **51.26%** |

These are anticipation (next-action prediction) top-1 numbers at τ=1s; the recognition accuracy of the internal single-frame classifier supporting AAG+ is **72.38–72.95%** on IKEA-ASM.[^1_7]

### Pose-Aware Weakly-Supervised Segmentation (CVPR 2025)

A CVPR 2025 workshop paper introduces pose-based contrastive learning to distill pose knowledge into RGB encoders for action segmentation on IKEA ASM, eliminating the need for pose features at inference time while achieving improvements across multiple segmentation frameworks.[^1_8][^1_9]

***

| Approach | Representative Methods | Top-1 Accuracy Range | Key Advantage |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| RGB only (3D CNN) | I3D, C3D, P3D | ~25–36% | Simple; no skeleton needed |

## Key Takeaways

- **Baseline accuracy** on IKEA ASM with classical methods (I3D single view) is approximately **30–35% frame-wise accuracy**.[^1_2][^1_4]
- **SOTA recognition accuracy** as of 2025–2026 reaches approximately **72–73% top-1** using a single RGB frame augmented with depth and semantic action history context (AAG+).[^1_7]
- Multi-view fusion alone gives a massive **+18% boost** over single-view RGB.[^1_5]
- Foundation models like **DinoV2 with self-attention fusion** are the strongest RGB-only approach, reaching **56.52% mean top-1**.[^1_3]
- Adding **depth + action history** context is far more impactful than pose keypoints alone, suggesting that semantic/procedural context — knowing what steps have already been completed — is the most informative modality for this structured assembly domain.[^1_7]

# HMR Human Mesh Recovery Kanazawa 2018 vs pose keypoint vector for action recognition.

What are the differences between using SMPL body model vs 2D/3D keypoints as
conditioning signal for activity understanding? Why choose sparse keypoints
over dense mesh for conditioning?

This is a deep and practically important question for your IKEA ASM work, Bashara. Here is a thorough technical breakdown.

***

## HMR (Kanazawa 2018) Recap

HMR is an end-to-end CVPR 2018 framework by Kanazawa, Black, Jacobs, and Malik that recovers a full 3D body mesh from a **single RGB image**. Rather than predicting 2D/3D keypoint coordinates directly, HMR regresses the parameters of the **SMPL** (Skinned Multi-Person Linear) model — specifically, shape parameters $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$, per-joint rotations $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^{23 \times 3}$, and global orientation $\psi$ — using an iterative regression loop with an adversarial discriminator trained on a large database of real 3D meshes. The output is a dense 3D mesh of **6,890 vertices** with full surface geometry.[^2_1][^2_2]

***

## SMPL Parameters vs. Keypoint Vectors

The two representations encode human pose in fundamentally different ways:


| Property | SMPL (HMR output) | 2D Keypoints | 3D Keypoints |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Representation** | Rotation matrices per joint + shape β | (x, y) pixel coords × N joints | (x, y, z) world/cam coords × N joints |
| **Dimensionality** | ~82–156 params (compact) | 2N (e.g., 34 for 17 joints) | 3N (e.g., 51 for 17 joints) |
| **Surface info** | Dense mesh (6,890 vertices) | None | None |
| **Body shape** | Explicit (β encodes height, weight, limb ratios) | Not encoded | Not encoded |
| **Depth** | Full 3D; depth inferred | Depth-ambiguous | Depth explicit |
| **Camera dependency** | Global orientation separable from body pose | Highly camera-dependent | Camera-relative (can be normalized) |
| **Occlusion handling** | **Amodal** — all joints always present | Missing joints = missing signal | Same as 2D, unless 3D lifted |
| **Downstream complexity** | Requires special rotation handling (6D repr.) | Trivial vector ops | Trivial vector ops |


***

## Why SMPL Is Richer — But Not Always Better for Conditioning

### The amodal advantage

SMPL meshes are **amodal** — because the body model is parametric, all joints and surface vertices are always reconstructed even under occlusion. Rajasegaran et al. (LART, CVPR 2023) explicitly demonstrate this: a person closing a door with an occluded hand is still correctly classified because the SMPL mesh reconstructs the arm geometry even though the hand is invisible to the 2D backbone.[^2_3]

### Body shape encodes identity context

The $\beta$ shape vector encodes body dimensions (height, limb proportions, torso width), which can disambiguate actions affected by scale — e.g., reaching vs. crouching differs depending on arm length relative to torso. Keypoint vectors carry no shape information whatsoever.[^2_2]

### 3D pose is viewpoint-invariant

SMPL separates global orientation $\psi$ from body-relative joint angles $\theta$, making $\theta$ a **view-invariant** pose descriptor. For action recognition across multiple camera views (like IKEA ASM's front/side/top cameras), this is a significant advantage over 2D keypoints, which are entirely camera-dependent.[^2_3]

***

## Why Sparse Keypoints Are Often Preferred for Action Recognition Conditioning

Despite SMPL's richer representation, sparse 3D keypoints are frequently chosen as conditioning signals for action recognition models. The core reasons are:

- **Dimensionality and simplicity**: 17 COCO keypoints = 51 floats. A dense SMPL mesh = 6,890 × 3 = 20,670 floats. Even SMPL pose parameters require careful handling of 3×3 rotation matrices and their continuity in neural networks.[^2_3]
- **Rotation representation brittleness**: Axis-angle and quaternion SMPL representations have known discontinuities that destabilize training. 6D rotation representations help  but add engineering complexity absent in simple (x, y, z) vectors.[^2_4]
- **GNN and transformer compatibility**: Graph neural networks and transformer architectures naturally ingest joint coordinate sequences as node/token features — a flat (N, 3) tensor is trivially tokenized. SMPL rotation tensors require additional projection layers.[^2_3]
- **Dense mesh is redundant for classification**: Surface shape (vertex positions between joints) contributes almost nothing to activity classification — what distinguishes "screwing" from "hammering" is limb trajectory, not skin surface geometry. Dense vertices add noise and computation with no discriminative benefit.
- **Training data scarcity**: SMPL requires full 3D pseudo-ground-truth (via HMR or optimization), which introduces error. 2D/3D keypoints can be reliably obtained from off-the-shelf detectors (MediaPipe, ViTPose, RTMPose) directly on in-the-wild footage.
- **Proven benchmark gains**: LART (CVPR 2023) showed that 2D keypoint baselines (PoTion: 13.1 mAP, JMRN: 14.1 mAP) were comprehensively beaten by SMPL-based 3D pose (22.3–24.1 mAP), but adding **explicit 3D joint coordinates on top of SMPL angles** (SMPL + Joints) further boosted performance, suggesting the two are complementary.[^2_3]

***

## Practical Design Recommendation for IKEA ASM

Given IKEA ASM's structured assembly context with fixed camera views and fine-grained hand/arm actions, the most pragmatic conditioning strategy is:

1. **Use 3D keypoints (17–33 joints)** as the primary pose conditioning signal — low-dimensional, robust, graph-friendly, and sufficient for capturing arm elevation, wrist orientation, and torso posture.
2. **Add SMPL joint angles** only if you need viewpoint invariance across IKEA ASM's 3 camera angles (front/side/top), since $\theta$ is camera-agnostic.
3. **Skip the dense mesh entirely** for the activity head — it adds 20K floats of surface geometry with negligible discriminative signal for action class boundaries like "spin," "place," or "pick up".[^2_5][^2_3]
4. **Consider hand keypoints** (21 per hand via MediaPipe or SMPL-X) specifically for IKEA ASM, since ~70% of atomic assembly actions involve fine-grained hand–object contact, which neither body-level keypoints nor SMPL body (non-X) captures well.[^2_6]

# Multi-task learning for simultaneous pose estimation and activity recognition.

Does training pose estimation jointly with activity classification improve both tasks
vs training each separately (single-task baseline)? What is the reported accuracy
gap between multi-task and single-task on assembly or action recognition benchmarks?

The evidence strongly supports that multi-task learning (MTL) for simultaneous pose estimation and action recognition improves **both** tasks compared to single-task baselines, but the magnitude and direction of the benefit is nuanced and architecture-dependent.

***

## The Core Mutual Benefit Mechanism

The intuition is that pose estimation and action recognition share a common **body-centric feature hierarchy** — both tasks need to understand joint positions, limb orientations, and kinematic chains. A shared backbone learning to localize joints necessarily encodes spatially structured body representations that make action classification more discriminative. Conversely, action supervision guides the pose encoder to prioritize **motion-relevant** body regions over static structure, improving temporal pose accuracy.[^3_1][^3_2]

***

## Quantitative Accuracy Gaps: MTL vs. Single-Task

The most directly comparable numbers come from Luvizon et al. (CVPR 2018 / IEEE TPAMI 2021 — the **DeepHAR** framework):[^3_3][^3_4]


| Setting | Penn Action Acc | NTU RGB+D Acc | Human3.6M MPJPE |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Single-task (action only) | 87.5% | — | — |
| Single-task (pose only) | — | — | 49.5 mm |
| Multi-task (same dataset) | 97.4% | — | 48.6 mm |
| Multi-task (+MPII +H36M) | **98.2%** | **89.9%** | **48.6 mm** |

Key findings from this work:[^3_4]

- **Action recognition** on Penn Action: +9.9% absolute accuracy gain from single-task to multi-task (87.5% → 97.4%).
- **3D pose estimation** on Human3.6M: +0.9 mm improvement in MPJPE (single-task 49.5mm → multi-task 48.6mm) — smaller but consistent.
- MTL outperformed previous NTU RGB+D SOTA by at least **+3.3% absolute**.

***

## Evidence Across Other Benchmarks

### Action + Gesture MTL (ICCV 2025 Workshop)

An ICCV 2025 study on joint action and gesture recognition (closely related to assembly fine-grained recognition) found that **almost all MTL configurations outperform their single-task learning (STL) variants** across multiple datasets, with the shared representation providing both efficiency and generalization benefits.[^3_5][^3_6]

### Human-Object Interaction (2025)

A 2025 HOI study using skeleton-based MTL with scene context reported **99.25% accuracy vs. 96.5%** for the skeleton-only single-task baseline — a **+2.75% absolute gain**.[^3_7]

### Body-Part-Aware MTL (Stanford 40 Actions)

A body-part-aware MTL framework achieved **91.91% mAP** vs. a single-task network at 91.2% mAP — a smaller **+0.71%** gain, with the paper noting the adaptive multi-task loss function kept the pose estimation performance drop minimal when adding the action head.[^3_8]

***

## Why Pose Estimation Also Improves Under MTL

This is the more surprising direction. Action supervision improves pose estimation for three reasons:[^3_9][^3_8]

- **Temporal regularization**: Action labels enforce consistency in joint trajectories over a clip, suppressing per-frame jitter that plagues single-frame pose estimators.
- **Semantic context**: Knowing the action class helps resolve kinematic ambiguity — a person "screwing" must have their wrist in a specific rotation range, constraining the pose search space.
- **Dataset mixing**: Multi-task training allows a single backbone to consume both pose-annotated data (e.g., MPII, COCO) and action-annotated data (NTU, Penn Action), effectively increasing useful training signal for the shared trunk.[^3_3][^3_4]

***

## When MTL Hurts: Task Conflict

MTL is not universally beneficial. Three documented failure modes are relevant to assembly action recognition:

- **Gradient interference**: If pose estimation gradients and action classification gradients point in conflicting directions for certain examples (e.g., ambiguous frames), naive loss summation can hurt both tasks.[^3_10]
- **Annotation imbalance**: Assembly datasets like IKEA ASM have dense pose annotations but sparse or coarse action labels — the pose task dominates the gradient signal and starves the action head.[^3_8]
- **Task granularity mismatch**: Pose estimation is frame-level; action recognition is clip-level. Architectures that don't decouple temporal resolution between heads (e.g., sharing a single-frame feature without temporal aggregation) see degraded action performance.[^3_9]

***

## Design Recommendations for IKEA ASM

Given your setup with IKEA ASM (pose keypoints + activity labels available), the practical playbook is:

- **Use hard parameter sharing** for the visual backbone (ResNet / ViT trunk), but apply **separate temporal aggregation heads** for pose (frame-level) and activity (clip-level).[^3_9]
- **Decouple the prediction branches**: Luvizon et al. specifically found that decoupling keypoint prediction from the action head (no gradient from action classification flowing through the heatmap decoder) consistently improved both tasks over naive end-to-end coupling.[^3_1][^3_4]
- **Apply uncertainty-weighted loss** (Kendall et al. style) rather than fixed $\lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_\text{pose} + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_\text{action}$ — this automatically balances the two loss magnitudes and reduces gradient conflict.[^3_10]
- Expect roughly a **+5–10% action accuracy** gain and **~1–2% pose MPJPE** improvement over single-task baselines, based on comparable assembly-like benchmarks.[^3_7][^3_4]

# IKEA ASM dataset exact frame count per activity class for all 16 assembly activities.

How many annotated frames does each activity have? What is the class distribution
imbalance ratio between the most common and rarest activity?
Source: Ben-Shabat et al. 2021 "IKEA Assembly in the Wild" IJCV paper.

There does not appear to be any publicly available table giving the *exact* number of annotated frames per class for the 16 assembly activities on IKEA ASM, nor an explicit numeric imbalance ratio, in Ben‑Shabat et al.’s ASM/IAW papers or their associated public materials. Based on what is available, I cannot reliably list per‑class frame counts or a precise most‑common/rarest activity ratio without reprocessing the raw annotations yourself.[^1_1][^1_2][^1_3]

## What the papers and docs actually state

- The IKEA ASM dataset has 371 assemblies, 3,046,977 RGB frames (about 35.27 hours), and 16,764 annotated *atomic actions*, with an average of 150 frames per action (about 6 seconds).[^1_3][^1_1]
- The authors explicitly note that the action distribution is highly imbalanced (e.g., leg‑assembly occurs four times per table), and they show this only as a bar chart (Figure 3), not as a numeric per‑class table.[^1_4][^1_1]
- The paper and arXiv version refer readers to “supplemental” material for the *list* of action names and IDs, but that material (at least in the public arXiv/ CVF/ project‑page mirrors) still does not expose a numeric table of per‑class frame counts or per‑class durations.[^1_5][^1_1]
- Follow‑up work that uses ASM (e.g., Zhao et al. 2022 PLOS One on compositional action recognition, and later HOI papers) cites the same aggregate statistics for ASM (16,764 actions, ~3M frames, ~150 frames/action) and discusses imbalance qualitatively, but likewise does not publish a 16‑class per‑activity frame‑count table.[^1_2][^1_3]

In other words, the literature and official project pages confirm total frames and annotated actions and show qualitative distribution plots, but stop short of giving the per‑class frame counts you are asking for.

## Why the 16‑activity frame counts are not easily available

- IKEA ASM’s *native* labeling is in terms of **33 fine‑grained atomic actions**, organized under higher‑level groupings (e.g., verbs, object types), and several later works introduce hierarchies (e.g., 4 high‑level categories above the 33 actions), but I could not locate any official resource that defines a canonical set of **16 assembly “activities”** *with published per‑class stats*.[^1_6][^1_1]
- The IJCV‑style extension of ASM and the “IKEA Assembly in the Wild (IAW)” dataset paper focus mainly on alignment between instructions and video segments and again use aggregate counts and qualitative plots rather than a numerical distribution table over the 16 higher‑level activities.[^1_7][^1_8][^1_9]
- The IKEA ASM GitHub repo provides code and points to Google Drive bundles for “Annotations – action”, but the annotations themselves (where you *could* compute exact counts) are distributed as large external archives; no pre‑computed class‑statistics file is exposed in the repo or on the project page.[^1_10][^1_5]

Given this, the only way (that I can see) to obtain the exact frame counts per activity and the exact imbalance ratio would be to process the official annotation files directly.

## How you could compute the exact per‑class frame counts

If you download the official IKEA ASM action annotations from the links referenced on the project page / GitHub, you can compute the frame counts yourself. At a high level:[^1_5][^1_10]

1. **Obtain action annotations**
    - From the IKEA ASM Google Drive, download the “Annotations – action” package (it is separate from the videos).[^1_5]
    - These annotations typically contain, for each assembly video (and view), a list of action segments with:
        - video ID / sequence ID
        - start frame index
        - end frame index
        - atomic action label ID (e.g., 0–32 for the 33 atomic classes)
2. **Decide what you mean by “frame count”**
    - If you want **per‑view** frame counts, you treat each camera view independently: for each segment, add $(\text{end} - \text{start} + 1)$ frames to that action’s class total.
    - If you want **unique time‑instants across all views**, you should instead work in timeline time (e.g., per assembly, per frame index) and count each time‑instant once, regardless of how many views see it.
3. **Map atomic actions to the 16 activities**
    - From the IJCV/WACV/IAW paper or its supplementary, extract or reconstruct the mapping from the 33 atomic actions to the 16 “assembly activities” (this mapping is described conceptually but not as a numeric table).[^1_1][^1_7]
    - Build a dictionary, e.g. `activity_id = f(atomic_action_id)`, so that each atomic action segment can be assigned to exactly one of the 16 activities.
4. **Aggregate frame counts**
    - For each annotated action segment:
        - Compute its frame length $L = \text{end_frame} - \text{start_frame} + 1$.
        - Map its atomic label to an activity label using your mapping.
        - Add $L$ to a running total for that activity.
    - After processing all segments, you have an exact per‑activity frame count:
        - `frames_per_activity[activity_id]`
5. **Compute the imbalance ratio**
    - Let $F_{\max}$ be the largest value in `frames_per_activity` and $F_{\min}$ be the smallest non‑zero value.
    - The imbalance ratio requested is simply

$$
\text{ratio} = \frac{F_{\max}}{F_{\min}}
$$
    - This gives the exact factor by which the most common activity has more annotated frames than the rarest.

A rough implementation sketch in Python (once you have the annotation files parsed) would look like:

```python
from collections import defaultdict

frames_per_activity = defaultdict(int)

for segment in all_segments:  # iterate over all annotated actions
    atomic_id = segment['atomic_action_id']
    activity_id = atomic_to_activity[atomic_id]  # your 33→16 mapping
    length = segment['end_frame'] - segment['start_frame'] + 1
    frames_per_activity[activity_id] += length

F_max = max(frames_per_activity.values())
F_min = min(v for v in frames_per_activity.values() if v > 0)
imbalance_ratio = F_max / F_min
```

# IKEA ASM dataset train validation test split. How many sequences and frames in each split?

How is the dataset divided for training and evaluation? What is the total number of
annotated keyframe samples used for model training vs testing?
Source: IKEA ASM dataset paper Ben-Shabat 2021.

I now have all the necessary information from the Ben-Shabat et al. paper to give a thorough, accurate answer.

The IKEA ASM dataset uses an **environment-based train/test split** — no separate validation set is defined in the original paper; it is a two-way split only.[^2_1][^2_2]

## Train / Test Split Design

The split is designed so that test environments do not appear in the training set and vice versa.  The authors chose an environment-based separation (rather than subject-based) because there is significant overlap between subjects across scenes, and a joint subject–environment hold-out would require discarding a large portion of the data.  Scripts are provided to generate alternative splits (by subject, environment, or both jointly).[^2_2][^2_1]

- **Train set**: 254 assemblies (scans) — environments 3, 4, and 5
- **Test set**: 117 assemblies (scans) — environments 1 (family room) and 2 (office)
- **Total**: 371 assemblies[^2_1][^2_2]


## Sequences and Frame Counts

| Split | Assemblies (scans) | Est. RGB Videos (×3 views) | Est. Frames |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Train | 254 | ~762 | ~2,085,000 |
| Test | 117 | ~351 | ~960,000 |
| **Total** | **371** | **1,113** | **3,046,977** |

The total frame count of **3,046,977 frames (~35.27 hours)** is reported directly, with an average of **2,735.2 frames per video (~1.89 min)**.  Since the paper does not publish per-split frame totals, the estimates above are derived from the average frames-per-video applied to each split's proportion of the 1,113 RGB videos (254 × 3 = 762 for train; 117 × 3 = 351 for test).[^2_2][^2_1]

## Annotated Actions

The dataset contains **16,764 annotated atomic actions** in total, with an average of **150 frames per action (~6 seconds)**.  The paper shows the class distribution across train and test sets as a bar chart (Figure 3), confirming that each action class contains **at least 20 clips** in each split, and that there is a high imbalance due to the repetitive nature of table leg assembly (four legs per table).  The exact per-split action counts are not broken out in a published table.[^2_1]

## Keyframe Annotations (Pose \& Segmentation)

For instance segmentation and human pose estimation, only **~1% of the frames were manually annotated** as keyframes — specifically selected to cover diverse object and human poses throughout each video.  This amounts to approximately **~30,470 keyframes** across the full dataset (1% of 3,046,977).  The paper does not publish the exact train vs. test keyframe counts, but notes:[^2_3][^2_1]

- **Train set** (3D pose): reprojection error of 7.12 pixels; **83% of ground-truth joints detected**[^2_1]
- **Test set** (3D pose): reprojection error of 9.14 pixels; **53% of ground-truth joints detected**[^2_1]

These figures (proportionally ~254/371 ≈ 68% train, ~117/371 ≈ 32% test) suggest roughly **~20,700 keyframes** in the train split and **~9,750 keyframes** in the test split for pose/segmentation tasks.

## No Validation Set in the Original Paper

The original WACV 2021 paper defines only train and test splits.  Some follow-up works (e.g., compositional action recognition studies) carve out their own validation subsets from the training portion using custom criteria (e.g., non-overlapping action labels).  One downstream study also applies a 70-10-20 train–validation–test subdivision.  These are researcher-defined and not part of the canonical Ben-Shabat et al. benchmark.[^2_4][^2_5][^2_2][^2_1]

# IKEA ASM dataset video statistics: total number of videos, frame rate, resolution,

number of IKEA furniture categories, number of human subjects, camera setups
(RGB, depth, top view). What furniture types are in the dataset?

I now have the full paper content. Here is a comprehensive breakdown of every dataset statistic reported by Ben-Shabat et al.

## Camera Setup

IKEA ASM uses **three Microsoft Kinect V2 cameras** capturing simultaneously in real time at **~24 fps**.  The three views are:[^3_1]

- **Front view** — eye-level height (~1.6 m), facing the assembler
- **Side view** — eye-level height (~1.6 m), orthogonal to front
- **Top view** — overhead, used also for the depth stream

Each Kinect V2 captures both RGB and depth. Only the **top-view camera** provides the depth video stream; the front and side cameras contribute RGB only.  This yields **1,113 RGB videos** and **371 depth videos** in total.[^3_1]

## Video and Frame Statistics

| Property | Value |
| :-- | :-- |
| Resolution | **1920 × 1080** (Full HD) [^3_1] |
| Frame rate | **~24 fps** [^3_1] |
| Total assemblies (scans) | **371** [^3_1] |
| Total RGB videos (×3 views) | **1,113** [^3_1] |
| Total depth videos (top view) | **371** [^3_1] |
| Total frames | **3,046,977 (~35.27 hours)** [^3_1] |
| Avg. frames per video | **2,735.2 (~1.89 min)** [^3_1] |
| Total annotated atomic actions | **16,764** [^3_1] |
| Avg. frames per action | **~150 (~6 seconds)** [^3_1] |
| Manually annotated keyframes | **~1% of all frames** (~30,470) [^3_1] |

## Furniture Categories

The dataset covers **4 IKEA furniture types** assembled in **3 color variants** (white, oak, and black):[^3_1]

1. **Side table**
2. **Coffee table**
3. **TV bench**
4. **Drawer unit**

The color variation is intentional — white parts are documented as the hardest to segment (high reflectance, low contrast against white work surfaces), while oak parts are the easiest.[^3_1]

## Human Subjects and Environments

- **48 human subjects** participated across all recording sessions[^3_1]
- **5 distinct environments** — offices, labs, and family homes — to ensure background diversity in layout, appearance, and lighting[^3_1]
- **10 camera configurations** in total (two per environment: floor assembly and table-surface assembly)[^3_1]
- The dynamic backgrounds contain moving people irrelevant to assembly, intentionally forcing models to focus on the assembler and furniture parts[^3_1]


## Annotations Detail

For each assembly video, the dataset provides:[^3_1]

- **Temporal action labels** — start and end frame boundaries for all atomic actions (from a pre-defined vocabulary), annotated via Amazon Mechanical Turk
- **Instance segmentation** — polygon-level object masks for furniture parts, manually labeled on ~1% of keyframes; pseudo ground-truth generated automatically for the rest via Mask R-CNN + PointRend
- **Part identity tracking** — unique IDs per furniture part, consistent across the full video
- **2D human pose** — **12 body joints + 5 face keypoints** (COCO format), with a confidence value (1–3) per joint, annotated for the same ~1% keyframes as segmentation[^3_1]
- **3D pseudo-ground-truth pose** — obtained by triangulating fine-tuned 2D detections across the three calibrated camera views (reprojection error: 7.12 px train / 9.14 px test)[^3_1]
- **Extrinsic camera calibration** — provided for all three views to enable 3D lifting and multi-view fusion[^3_1]

# Multi-task learning training strategy for pose estimation plus activity classification.

What batch size, learning rate, optimizer (SGD vs Adam), weight decay, and number
of epochs are typical for ResNet-50 fine-tuning on assembly datasets with under
10,000 samples? What data augmentation techniques prevent overfitting?

For a ResNet‑50 multi‑task head doing pose + activity on an assembly dataset with <10k labeled samples, you generally want small batches (8–32), conservative learning rates (∼1e‑3 for new heads and 1e‑4–3e‑4 for the backbone), SGD or AdamW with weight decay around 1e‑4, and relatively long training (≈80–120 epochs) plus strong geometric/photometric + occlusion augmentations to control overfitting.[^1_1][^1_2][^1_3][^1_4]

Below are concrete, literature‑grounded defaults and how to tweak them.

## Typical hyperparameters

Empirically, multi‑task pose+action models on human datasets are commonly trained with small batches (16–32) and low learning rates using Adam/RMSprop/SGD, especially when data is limited. A reasonable starting configuration for <10k assembly samples is:[^1_2][^1_5][^1_1]


| Component | Recommended setting (ResNet‑50, RGB frames 256–384 px) |
| :-- | :-- |
| Batch size | 16 (safe range 8–32) |
| Optimizer | SGD + momentum 0.9 (or AdamW as alternative) |
| LR – heads only | 1e‑3 (Adam/AdamW) or 3e‑3 (SGD) |
| LR – full model | 1e‑4–3e‑4 for backbone, 3× higher for heads |
| Weight decay | 1e‑4 (SGD/AdamW), 1e‑5–1e‑4 if plain Adam |
| Epochs | 80–120 total with early stopping |

### Why these numbers?

- A body‑part‑aware multi‑task pose+action model based on HRNet and ResNet18 trains with batch size 16 and Adam at lr 1e‑5 on small action datasets, showing a trend toward small batches and low learning rates for multi‑task vision on limited data.[^1_2]
- A real‑time multi‑task 2D/3D pose + action model trains pose with batch size 32 and RMSprop at lr 1e‑3, then continues multi‑task training with the same order of magnitude learning rate and modest epochs, again reflecting small‑batch, low‑LR practice.[^1_1]
- A multi‑task human activity repo first trains new FC heads on a pre‑trained CNN with batch size 64 and Adam lr 0.01 for 100 epochs, then fine‑tunes the base model with much smaller lrs (5e‑5–2e‑5), illustrating the common “high‑lr heads, low‑lr backbone” pattern.[^1_5]
- Modern fine‑tuning work on subsets of large datasets typically uses SGD with momentum, lr around 1e‑3 and weight decay 1e‑4, with long schedules (hundreds of epochs) but heavy regularization.[^1_6][^1_7]

Given your dataset is significantly smaller than ImageNet/Kinetics, you want to be closer to the low‑LR, strong‑regularization side of these ranges.

## Optimizer: SGD vs Adam

Multi‑task pose+action papers use a mix of adaptive methods and SGD: RMSprop for pose, then SGD with momentum for certain action stages, and joint training with RMSprop again for stability. Another multi‑task pose+action model trains entirely with Adam.[^1_1][^1_2]

Practical guidance:

- Use **SGD + momentum 0.9** if:
    - You care about best test‑time generalization, and can afford a bit more tuning.
    - You can use cosine or step LR decay and maybe a warm‑up phase.[^1_8][^1_7][^1_6]
- Use **AdamW** if:
    - You want faster, more forgiving optimization on a very small dataset.
    - Start around lr 1e‑4–3e‑4 for the backbone and 3e‑4–1e‑3 for task heads.[^1_5][^1_2]

In both cases, **weight decay ≈1e‑4** is a good default, consistent with modern ResNet training and fine‑tuning work.[^1_7][^1_6]

## Training schedule and epochs

Multi‑task pose+action work typically does staged training: pose‑only, then action‑only, then joint, with total effective epochs in the tens to low hundreds on medium‑sized datasets. Small‑data action recognition fine‑tuning commonly runs 50–100+ epochs when using augmentation and regularization.[^1_6][^1_5][^1_1]

For <10k assembly samples:

1. **Stage 1 – heads only (stabilization)**
    - Freeze ResNet‑50 backbone; train pose and activity heads.
    - Optimizer: AdamW or SGD.
    - LR: 1e‑3 for AdamW, 3e‑3 for SGD.
    - Epochs: 10–20, monitor val loss.
2. **Stage 2 – partial fine‑tune (last 1–2 stages of ResNet)**
    - Unfreeze layers from conv4_x upward (or conv3_x if you need more capacity).
    - LR (backbone): 1e‑4–3e‑4; heads: 3× backbone LR.
    - Decay: cosine decay to 1e‑6, or step down by 10× at 60% and 80% of training.[^1_8][^1_6]
    - Epochs: bring total to ≈80–120, with early stopping on a held‑out subject/environment split (as used in IKEA ASM).[^1_9]
3. **Optional Stage 3 – full fine‑tune**
    - If still underfitting, unfreeze the full backbone but drop LR by 2–3× and run another 20–40 epochs.

This schedule mirrors multi‑stage multi‑task training used in pose+action work (pose pretraining, then action, then joint), which was shown to improve stability and accuracy on both tasks.[^1_1]

## Loss weighting for multi‑task

Multi‑task pose+action architectures typically use a weighted sum of pose regression loss and action classification loss, sometimes with staged decoupling of pose heads to prevent task interference.[^1_2][^1_1]

For your case:

- Use a total loss:
$L = L_{\text{pose}} + \lambda\,L_{\text{activity}}$ (elastic‑net or L1/L2 for pose; cross‑entropy for activity).[^1_1]
- Start with **λ ≈ 0.5–1.0** and watch for:
    - Pose metrics dropping when activity improves → decrease λ.
    - Activity lagging while pose is very strong → increase λ or add a short activity‑only fine‑tune stage (freeze pose head, optimize classification tail), as done in some multi‑task works.[^1_2][^1_1]


## Data augmentation to prevent overfitting

Because assembly scenes feature self‑occlusion, clutter, and viewpoint variation, you want augmentations that reflect those realities. Surveys show that geometric transforms (flips, rotation, scaling, cropping), color transforms, and occlusion‑style augmentations significantly improve ResNet‑type models on diverse image tasks.[^1_3][^1_10][^1_11]

### Image‑level augmentations (RGB)

Use these for both pose and activity heads:

- **Geometric**
    - Random horizontal flip (p≈0.5); safe for most assembly actions where left/right symmetry holds.[^1_10][^1_3]
    - Small random rotation (±10–15°) and scaling (0.8–1.2) to simulate camera and subject variation.[^1_11][^1_3]
    - Random resized crop (e.g., 80–100% of area, aspect ratio 3/4–4/3), then resize back. This is standard in modern ResNet training and fine‑tuning pipelines.[^1_3][^1_7]
- **Photometric**
    - Color jitter: random brightness/contrast/saturation/hue changes (small, e.g. ±0.2–0.4) to account for varying lighting in different assembly environments.[^1_11][^1_3]
    - Optional Gaussian noise or slight motion blur to mimic sensor noise.
- **Occlusion / robustness**
    - **Random Erasing / Cutout**: randomly erase a rectangular patch in the image (with random values or mean color). This has been shown to reduce overfitting and improve robustness in classification, detection, and person re‑ID.[^1_4][^1_12]
    - Mild versions (small max area like 5–15%) are usually safe; you can ramp this up if you see strong overfitting.


### Pose / skeleton‑level augmentations

If your multi‑task setup consumes explicit keypoints or pose heatmaps into the activity branch, skeleton‑space augmentation helps a lot on limited data, and has been shown to improve real‑world skeleton‑based activity recognition by modeling realistic noise and occlusion.[^1_13][^1_14]

Consider:

- Random joint jitter (small Gaussian noise in pixel or normalized coordinates).
- Randomly dropping joints or entire body parts (head, arms, legs) for short subsequences to mimic occlusions, as proposed for skeleton‑based action recognition.[^1_14][^1_13]
- Short frame‑wise occlusion: zero out all joints for a small random subsequence, again following skeleton augmentation methods.[^1_13]


### Temporal augmentations (for activity clips)

Most multi‑task pose+action models work on short RGB clips and report improvements from sampling diverse segments within each action instance.[^1_15][^1_1]

For assembly:

- Randomly sample the **start frame and temporal stride** within an action segment (e.g., stride 1–3 frames) so the network sees early, middle, and late phases of each assembly action.[^1_15][^1_1]
- Randomly drop or mask a small proportion of frames in the clip (temporal dropout) to make the classifier robust to dropped frames and jitter.[^1_13]
- Avoid temporal reversal for assembly (ordering matters for many steps).

# Assembly activity recognition failure cases and limitations. What activities are

hardest to classify in IKEA assembly? Does occlusion by furniture parts cause
systematic keypoint detection failures? What is the typical worst-case confusion
in assembly action recognition (which pairs of activities get confused most)?

Assembly activity recognition on IKEA ASM is fundamentally harder than general action recognition due to fine-grained, visually similar actions performed in a static background, severe occlusion from furniture parts, and extreme class imbalance. Here is a thorough breakdown of every documented failure mode.

***

## Why the Dataset Is Structurally Hard

Three properties of furniture assembly push classifiers toward specific failure patterns:[^2_1]

- **Background invariance**: All actions happen in the same fixed scene, so the background gives zero discriminative signal. Models trained on YouTube-style datasets (Kinetics, HMDB) rely heavily on scene context, and that shortcut disappears entirely here.
- **Symmetric and visually near-identical parts**: Table legs, drawer panels, shelf boards, and bolts are textureless, same-color, and geometrically similar. A front drawer panel and a rear drawer panel look essentially the same to a convolutional detector.[^2_2][^2_1]
- **Actions defined by sub-centimeter state changes**: Whether a leg is being *spun in*, *aligned*, or *tightened* is determined by millimeter-level torque and contact geometry — information that RGB alone cannot recover without long temporal context.[^2_3][^2_1]

***

## Hardest Action Classes to Classify

In IKEA ASM, the action taxonomy includes **spinning in, aligning, tightening, attaching, and flipping** furniture components. The hardest cluster is the triplet **{align leg → spin leg → tighten leg}**. These actions share identical limb motion direction (rotation about a vertical axis), the same effector (fingers on a leg bolt), and nearly the same spatial region of the image. The only discriminators are rotational speed, the degree of contact, and whether the part is already threaded — none of which is cleanly visible frame-by-frame.[^2_4][^2_1][^2_3]

The dataset also suffers from **severe class imbalance**: because each table has four legs, *spin leg* appears **four times more often** than any unique assembly step, causing frame-accuracy metrics to heavily reward classifying everything as *spin leg* and inflating reported scores while recall on minority classes collapses. The macro-recall metric was added precisely because standard frame accuracy masks this pathology.[^2_1][^2_4]

Benchmark numbers underscore the difficulty. Frame-wise ResNet-50 achieves only about **33–35% frame accuracy** on IKEA ASM in most single-view settings, while I3D reaches ~57% compared to 68% on Kinetics — a meaningful drop on a dataset that should, in principle, be simpler. On the compositional action recognition split, ResNet-50 drops to **29.4% top-1** accuracy. 3D point-cloud methods that add full geometry (pose + depth) push accuracy to ~52%, confirming that the bottleneck is not model capacity but sensory completeness.[^2_5][^2_4][^2_1]

A related, cross-dataset finding from HA-ViD (an industrial assembly benchmark) confirms this pattern: **recognizing which objects and tools are being manipulated is consistently harder than recognizing the action verb** (e.g., knowing that a hand is "screwing" is easier than knowing it is screwing a *specific nut onto a specific bolt* in the right hole).[^2_6]

***

## Systematic Keypoint Detection Failures from Occlusion

Pose estimation collapses on IKEA ASM in a way that is structurally different from general datasets. The documented numbers are stark:

- **OpenPose**: PCK of 88.5% on MPII → **52.6% on IKEA ASM** (a ~36 point drop).[^2_1]
- **VIBE (3D)**: PA-MPJPE of 41.4 mm on Human3.6M → **940 mm on IKEA ASM** — a 22× error increase, indicating the model is almost entirely failing to localize body geometry.[^2_1]
- **Best fine-tuned model (Mask R-CNN ResNet-50)**: PCK@10 px = **64.3%**, AUC = 87.8, MPJPE = 11.5 px — the best result on this dataset still represents substantial failure on roughly one-third of joints.[^2_1]

The occlusion pattern is **systematic, not random**, with four documented mechanisms:

1. **Table surface lower-body occlusion**: In half the sessions subjects assemble on a table worksurface, which occludes feet, ankles, knees and often hips from the front and side cameras. Error analysis from IKEA ASM directly attributes lower-body keypoints being consistently less accurately detected than upper-body ones to this cause.[^2_1]
2. **Furniture parts occluding hands and wrists**: While a subject reaches under a shelf or between legs to tighten a bolt, the furniture part itself covers the hands — the most assembly-relevant joints. This is the primary source of self-plus-object occlusion.[^2_7][^2_1]
3. **Floor assembly postures**: When assembly is conducted on the floor (half of sessions), subjects crouch, kneel, or lie prone. These poses are almost entirely absent from COCO/MPII training distributions, causing pre-trained detectors to misidentify limb topology.[^2_1]
4. **Subject-specific demographic confounds**: Female subjects are detected *considerably less accurately* than male subjects in IKEA ASM, accounting for almost all entirely missed detections, likely due to differences in clothing style (baggy clothing, long hair) against the COCO training distribution.[^2_1]

From a tracking perspective, the side-view (family room) environment shows **high false negative rates** because the assembler's body directly occludes furniture parts being worked on, and the tracker assigns new IDs after long occlusions, inflating identity switches.[^2_1]

***

## Worst-Case Confusion Pairs

While IKEA ASM does not publish a canonical full confusion matrix in the main paper, the literature consistently identifies these high-confusion pairs:


| Confused Pair | Root Cause |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Spin leg ↔ Tighten leg** | Nearly identical hand motion (wrist rotation); distinguished only by torque level and whether threading is complete.[^2_1][^2_4] |
| **Align leg ↔ Spin leg** | Both involve bringing a leg to the table body; difference is contact state (pre-thread vs. threading).[^2_1][^2_3] |
| **Allen key screwing ↔ Screwdriver screwing** | Rotational wrist kinematics are nearly identical; tool identity is the only discriminator and tools are partially occluded during use.[^2_8] |
| **Attaching shelf ↔ Attaching drawer panel** | Front vs. rear panel confusion is the documented Mask R-CNN instance segmentation failure mode; the same ambiguity propagates into action classifiers.[^2_1][^2_2] |
| **Null/idle ↔ Transition actions** | Boundary frames between actions are ambiguous; HA-ViD shows that "null" (pause/idle) achieves high recall but low precision due to class dominance, and gets systematically over-predicted by classifiers addressing imbalance.[^2_6] |

In 3D point-cloud action recognition on IKEA ASM, applying bidirectional temporal context patches helps *most* action classes but shows **varied impact** across the spin/align/tighten cluster, indicating these remain resistant to even temporal reasoning improvements.[^2_4]

***

## Structural Implications for Your Multi-Task Model

Given these failure modes, a few targeted interventions follow directly:

- **Temporal context is mandatory** for the spin/align/tighten cluster. A frame-wise ResNet-50 without any temporal head will conflate all three. You need at minimum a short-term temporal window (8–16 frames) or a recurrent/3D head on the activity branch.[^2_4][^2_1]
- **Pose heatmaps should not be blindly trusted in lower-body regions** during table-assembly segments — consider masking or down-weighting lower limb joints in your pose loss using a per-joint confidence flag (IKEA ASM provides a 1–3 confidence score per annotated keypoint).[^2_1]
- **Class imbalance handling is critical**: the *spin leg* dominance will cause your activity head to shortcut to it. A weighted sampler (weight inversely proportional to class frequency) is already standard for IKEA ASM baselines and is essential for recovering minority-class recall.[^2_1]
- **Multi-view fusion recovers ~18 percentage points** of top-1 accuracy over single-view on IKEA ASM, suggesting that if you have access to multiple calibrated views (IKEA ASM provides three), fusing softmax scores across views is one of the highest-leverage improvements available.[^2_9][^2_5]
- **Object-interaction features matter more than pose alone** for distinguishing spin/tighten/align, since those actions differ in the *state of the part*, not in body configuration. Incorporating part segmentation confidence or a tracked object state alongside pose into the activity head is a promising mitigation.[^2_3][^2_1]

# PCKh metric definition for human pose estimation evaluation. What is PCKh@0.5?

How is the head segment length computed? How does PCKh differ from MPJPE
(mean per joint position error)? What is a good PCKh score on IKEA ASM?

PCKh is the standard 2D pose evaluation metric on MPII and assembly datasets, measuring what fraction of predicted joints land within a distance threshold normalized by the subject's **head segment length**, making it scale-invariant across subjects and image resolutions. Here is every component defined precisely.

***

## PCK: The Base Definition

The Percentage of Correct Keypoints (PCK) counts a predicted joint as *correct* if its Euclidean distance from the ground-truth joint is within a threshold $\tau$:[^3_1][^3_5]

$$
\text{PCK} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\!\left[\| \hat{p}_i - p_i \|_2 \leq \tau \right]
$$

where $\hat{p}_i$ is the predicted 2D location of joint $i$, $p_i$ is the ground truth, and the indicator $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ gives 1 if the condition holds, 0 otherwise. The result is averaged per joint and then across joints to give a single number between 0 and 1 (reported as a percentage).[^3_14][^3_1]

***

## PCKh: Head-Normalized Threshold

Plain PCK uses a fixed-pixel or torso-normalized threshold, which disadvantages smaller or more distant subjects. **PCKh** solves this by setting the threshold as a **fraction of the head segment length** — the Euclidean distance between the annotated *head-top* keypoint and the *upper-neck* keypoint in the skeleton:[^3_6][^3_9][^3_10]

$$
\tau_{\text{PCKh}} = \alpha \cdot \| p_{\text{head-top}} - p_{\text{upper-neck}} \|_2
$$

This makes the threshold **proportional to each subject's head size in the image**, automatically accounting for scale variation.[^3_10][^3_6]

### PCKh@0.5

**PCKh@0.5** sets $\alpha = 0.5$, meaning a joint is considered correct if it falls within **half the head segment length** of the ground truth:[^3_6][^3_9][^3_14]

$$
\text{PCKh@0.5}: \quad \tau = 0.5 \times \| p_{\text{head-top}} - p_{\text{upper-neck}} \|_2
$$

This is the dominant benchmark threshold on MPII Human Pose. It is tighter than it might initially seem — for a person whose head-to-neck distance subtends ~30 px in the image, the acceptance radius is only 15 px. State-of-the-art models on MPII achieve PCKh@0.5 of **90–94%** overall, with wrists and ankles typically 5–10 points below head and shoulder scores.[^3_2][^3_10]

***

## Head Segment Length: Exact Computation

In the MPII skeleton (16 joints), the head segment is defined as the segment connecting:

- **Joint 9** — upper neck (top of neck / base of head)
- **Joint 10** — head top (top of skull)

The head segment length $h$ is therefore:

$$
h = \| p_{\text{joint 10}} - p_{\text{joint 9}} \|_2 \quad \text{(pixels)}
$$

For PCKh@0.5 the acceptance radius is $0.5h$. Note this is **per-person, per-image** — a small person in a crowded scene gets a smaller threshold, and a large close-up subject gets a larger one, which is the entire point of the normalization.[^3_6][^3_9][^3_10]

***

## PCKh vs. MPJPE

These two metrics measure fundamentally different things and are used at different stages of your pipeline:


| Property | PCKh@0.5 | MPJPE |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Dimension** | 2D image space | 3D world/camera space (mm) |
| **Output** | % joints within threshold (higher = better) | Mean Euclidean distance in mm (lower = better) |
| **Normalization** | Threshold scaled by head size | Centroids of predicted and GT skeletons aligned (root joint alignment) |
| **Sensitivity** | Treats all errors inside threshold equally (binary) | Continuous — differentiates between 5 mm and 50 mm errors |
| **Typical benchmark** | MPII Human Pose (2D) | Human3.6M, MPI-INF-3DHP (3D) |
| **Failure mode** | Inflated by easy joints (head, torso) dragging up average | Sensitive to global depth errors |

In short, **PCKh is a binary correctness rate for 2D** — it tells you whether the joint is *in the right region*, while **MPJPE is a continuous distance error for 3D** — it tells you *by how much* you are off in metric space.  For IKEA ASM the MPJPE is reported after aligning the centroids of 3D point sets (as in Human3.6M), and uses a 150 mm fixed threshold for the 3D PCK variant rather than a head-normalized one.[^3_3][^3_6][^3_7][^3_8]

***

## What Is a Good PCKh Score on IKEA ASM?

IKEA ASM uses **PCK @ 10 pixels** (not PCKh@0.5) for 2D evaluation — a fixed-pixel threshold — because annotators standardized image crops. This is an important nuance: at typical subject scale in IKEA ASM frames, 10 px is roughly equivalent to ~0.2–0.3× head segment length, making it a *tighter* criterion than standard PCKh@0.5.[^3_8]

The published baseline scores are:[^3_8]


| Model | PCK @ 10 px | MPJPE (2D, px) | AUC |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| OpenPose (no fine-tune) | 52.6% | — | — |
| Mask R-CNN (no fine-tune) | ~55% | — | — |
| **Mask R-CNN (fine-tuned on IKEA ASM)** | **64.3%** | **11.5 px** | **87.8** |

For the 3D evaluation, the best model achieves PA-MPJPE ~940 mm *without* fine-tuning (model trained only on Human3.6M), which drops dramatically to usable levels only after domain adaptation.  A **PCK@10 px above 65–70%** is currently state-of-the-art territory on IKEA ASM, meaning there is substantial headroom compared to the 90%+ PCKh@0.5 achievable on MPII — a direct consequence of the occlusion and out-of-distribution pose challenges detailed in the previous discussion.[^3_8]

What is the accuracy improvement from adding FiLM conditioning on visual features

## What is available on IKEA ASM

- The original IKEA‑ASM dataset paper benchmarks standard RGB‑only and multi‑modal baselines (RGB, depth, pose, segmentation), but does not introduce FiLM or pose‑conditioned modulation; multimodality is handled in more conventional ways.[^1_2]

    - RGB‑only,
    - pose‑only,
    - RGB+pose by concatenation,

The only safe statement is qualitative and extrapolated from related work:

- RGB‑only < pose‑only (or pose+objects) < RGB+pose (concatenation/late fusion) on IKEA‑ASM for fine‑grained assembly understanding tasks.[^1_4][^1_1]
- FiLM has been shown in other domains to outperform naïve concatenation for conditional feature fusion, but that effect has **not** been quantified on IKEA‑ASM in current public work.[^1_3]

# Wing Loss vs L1 vs L2 vs Huber loss for body keypoint detection accuracy comparison.

What is the PCKh improvement when using Wing Loss instead of MSE/L2 for body
pose keypoint regression? Numerical comparison table if available.

I now have all the primary source data needed. Let me build the response with complete numerical tables directly from the papers.

**Critical clarification upfront:** The Feng et al. CVPR 2018 Wing Loss paper does **not** report PCKh or body keypoint results — it was designed for **facial landmark localization** only, evaluated with NME (Normalized Mean Error) on AFLW and 300W datasets.  The PCKh metric belongs to body pose estimation (MPII benchmark), which is covered by a separate line of work. Below are the full numerical comparisons from the actual papers.[^2_1][^2_2]

***

## Wing Loss: What Feng et al. CVPR 2018 Actually Measures

Wing Loss is a piecewise loss function defined as:

$$
\text{wing}(x) = \begin{cases} w \ln\left(1 + |x|/\epsilon\right) & \text{if } |x| < w \\ |x| - C & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

where $C = w - w\ln(1 + w/\epsilon)$ ensures continuity.  For small/medium errors, it uses a log function (larger gradient near zero than L1 or L2), and reverts to L1 for large errors to avoid outlier sensitivity.  The key insight: L1 and L2 both have their gradients dominated by large errors, neglecting the small corrections that matter most for accurate localization.[^2_3][^2_4][^2_1]

The paper evaluates on **AFLW** (19 facial landmarks, 4,386 test images) and **300W** (68 facial landmarks), measuring **Normalized Mean Error (NME)** — not PCKh.[^2_1]

***

## AFLW-Full: Loss Function Ablation (NME ×10⁻², lower is better)

The first core table from Feng et al. using a simple CNN-6 (5-conv + 1-FC) baseline:


| Method | L2 (MSE) | L1 | Smooth L1 (Huber) | Wing Loss |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **CNN-6** | 2.41 | 2.00 | 2.02 | **1.88** |
| CNN-6 + PDB | 2.23 | 1.89 | 1.91 | **1.83** |
| CNN-6/7 (two-stage) | 2.06 | 1.82 | 1.84 | **1.71** |
| CNN-6/7 + PDB | 1.94 | 1.73 | 1.76 | **1.65** |

PDB = Pose-Based Data Balancing (augmentation for rare pose samples).  Wing Loss achieves **6% lower error than Smooth L1** and **22% lower error than L2** at the CNN-6 level, improving from 2.41 (L2) → 1.88 (Wing).[^2_3]

| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |

## The Adaptive Wing Loss Extension (AWing, ICCV 2019)

Wang et al. extended Wing Loss to **heatmap regression** (not direct coordinate regression), creating Adaptive Wing Loss:[^2_7][^2_8]

$$
\text{AWing}(x) = \begin{cases} \omega \ln\left(1 + |x/\epsilon|^{2-y}\right) & \text{if } |x| < \theta \\ A|x| - C & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

where $y$ is the ground truth heatmap pixel value (adapting loss shape per-pixel). AWing was validated on body pose estimation (MPII) as a secondary task and showed improvement over standard MSE heatmap loss, though specific body PCKh delta numbers depend on backbone.[^2_8][^2_7]

# Class-balanced focal loss vs standard cross-entropy for activity recognition

on imbalanced datasets. What accuracy improvement does CB-Focal give over
standard CE on assembly activity datasets with class imbalance?
Cui et al. CVPR 2019 class-balanced loss results.

**Important clarification first:** Cui et al. CVPR 2019 evaluates exclusively on image classification benchmarks (CIFAR, iNaturalist, ImageNet) — **not** on assembly activity recognition or IKEA ASM.  The IKEA ASM dataset paper separately acknowledges high class imbalance and opts for macro-recall as a primary metric, but does not apply CB loss.  All numerical tables below are sourced directly from the original Cui et al. paper.[^3_1]

***

## The CB Loss Framework

The core idea: instead of re-weighting by inverse class frequency (which over-penalizes large classes), Cui et al. define the **effective number of samples** $E_n$ for each class as:

$$
E_n = \frac{1 - \beta^n}{1 - \beta}, \quad \beta = \frac{N-1}{N}
$$

where $n$ is the number of raw samples and $N$ is the number of unique prototypes. The class-balanced weight for class $i$ is then $\alpha_i \propto 1/E_{n_i}$.

This is applied to any underlying loss — softmax CE, sigmoid CE, or focal loss — yielding three variants: **CB-Softmax**, **CB-Sigmoid**, and **CB-Focal**:

$$
\text{CB-Focal}(z,y) = -\frac{1-\beta}{1-\beta^{n_y}} \sum_{i=1}^{C}(1-p_i^t)^\gamma \log(p_i^t)
$$

The key hyperparameter $\beta \in [0,1)$ smoothly interpolates between no re-weighting ($\beta=0$) and full inverse class frequency ($\beta \to 1$).

***

## Applying This to Assembly Activity Recognition (IKEA ASM)

The original IKEA ASM benchmark explicitly reports that the action class distribution is **highly imbalanced** and evaluates both frame-wise accuracy (FA) and macro-recall as metrics for this reason.  The following table shows which metrics matter most depending on your goal:[^3_1]


| Metric | Behavior with class imbalance | Why it matters for IKEA ASM |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Frame-wise accuracy (FA) | Biased toward frequent assembly phases | Can be misleadingly high |
| Macro-recall | Equally weights each class, penalizes missed rare actions | Preferred for imbalanced assembly |
| mAP | Captures precision/recall tradeoff per class | Best for multi-label overlap |

The IKEA ASM paper does not apply CB-Focal — it uses standard CE with I3D, P3D, C3D, and frame-based baselines.  Based on the Cui et al. results, applying CB-Focal to IKEA ASM would be most beneficial for **macro-recall on rare assembly phases** (rare pick-up, positioning steps), where improvements analogous to the 3–7 pp gains on iNaturalist can be expected. The exact delta would depend on the empirical imbalance ratio of the specific IKEA ASM action partition being used.[^3_1]

***

## Key Design Takeaways

- **Vanilla focal loss ≠ class-balanced focal loss**: Focal loss alone can hurt performance on long-tailed data by amplifying noise from rare classes; the CB weight is essential
- **Optimal β depends on dataset granularity**: Coarse tasks (few classes) → β=0.9999; fine-grained tasks (many similar classes) → β=0.9–0.99
- **CB-Focal is complementary to hard example mining**: The paper explicitly shows CB (class-frequency-based) and focal (difficulty-based) address orthogonal aspects of imbalance
- **No assembly dataset baseline in original paper**: Any claim of specific PCKh-style or accuracy gains on IKEA ASM from CB-Focal would need your own ablation — the original Cui et al. paper does not contain activity recognition benchmarks[^3_1]

# Kendall multi-task uncertainty weighting vs fixed lambda weights for pose

estimation and activity recognition joint training. How much does learned
task uncertainty weighting improve over manually tuned fixed weights?
Kendall Gal Cipolla CVPR 2018 homoscedastic uncertainty results table.

I have the complete paper. Here is the full numerical breakdown from every table and figure in Kendall, Gal \& Cipolla CVPR 2018.

***

## What the Paper Actually Tests

**Critical context first:** Kendall et al. CVPR 2018 evaluates on **scene geometry and semantics** on the CityScapes dataset — specifically three tasks: semantic segmentation (IoU%), instance segmentation (mean error px), and depth regression (mean inverse depth error px).  The paper does **not** report PCKh or body keypoint results. However, the framework directly generalizes to pose estimation + activity recognition joint training, which is why it is the dominant citation for that setup.[^4_1]

***

## Theoretical Foundation

The multi-task loss derived from maximizing a Gaussian likelihood with homoscedastic uncertainty is:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{total}(W, \sigma_1, \sigma_2) = \frac{1}{2\sigma_1^2}\mathcal{L}_1(W) + \frac{1}{\sigma_2^2}\mathcal{L}_2(W) + \log\sigma_1 + \log\sigma_2
$$

For mixed regression + classification (e.g., pose regression + activity recognition):

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2\sigma_1^2}\mathcal{L}_\text{pose}(W) + \frac{1}{\sigma_2^2}\mathcal{L}_\text{cls}(W) + \log\sigma_1 + \log\sigma_2
$$

where $\sigma_i$ are **learned** (not fixed) uncertainty parameters. In practice, the log-variance $s_i = \log \sigma_i^2$ is regressed for numerical stability — avoiding division by zero and allowing unconstrained scalar optimization.  As task noise $\sigma_i$ increases, the contribution of $\mathcal{L}_i$ decreases, automatically down-weighting tasks the network is uncertain about.

## Learned Weight Dynamics

At the **end of training**, the uncertainty parameters converge to a ratio:

$$
\sigma_\text{seg}^2 : \sigma_\text{depth}^2 : \sigma_\text{inst}^2 = 43 : 1 : 0.16
$$

meaning the loss weights become approximately **1/86 : 1/2 : 1/0.32**, i.e., segmentation contributes the smallest per-unit loss weight (~1/86), depth moderate (~1/2), and instance the largest (~3×).  This ratio — spanning **270×** from lowest to highest** — is virtually impossible to find by manual grid search, which explains why learned weights outperform even "approximately optimal" manual tuning.

***

## Applying This to Pose + Activity Recognition

The Kendall et al. framework applies directly to joint pose regression + activity classification (your IKEA ASM use case) via the mixed regression-classification objective (Equation 10 of the paper):

$$
\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{2\sigma_1^2}\mathcal{L}_\text{keypoint-reg} + \frac{1}{\sigma_2^2}\mathcal{L}_\text{activity-cls} + \log\sigma_1 + \log\sigma_2
$$

Practical expectations based on the paper's results:[^4_2]


| Risk scenario | Likely effect |
| :-- | :-- |
| Naive equal weights (λ=0.5/0.5) | May hurt both tasks; analogous to the 50.1% unweighted case |
| Manually tuned fixed λ | Better, but expensive; sensitive to keypoint scale (meters vs pixels vs normalized) |
| Uncertainty learned λ | Adapts to measurement scale mismatch between regression (px) and classification (nats); dynamically adjusts during training |
| Adding a 3rd task (e.g., object detection) | Learned weights scale gracefully; manual grid search becomes O(n²) expensive |

One downstream work, CertainPose (using Kendall's framework for multi-person pose estimation), confirmed that task-uncertainty weighting speeds up convergence and improves final accuracy without increasing computational overhead, validating the framework beyond the original scene understanding tasks.[^4_2]

# Which pose estimator was used to generate skeleton keypoints for IKEA ASM dataset?

Was OpenPose, HRNet, ViTPose, or another method used to extract 17-joint COCO-format
skeleton from IKEA assembly videos? How were the 3D coordinates (x,y,z) obtained
from monocular or multi-view video?

For IKEA ASM, the “ground‑truth” human poses are **not** generated with OpenPose, HRNet, ViTPose, etc. The 2D COCO‑format keypoints are manually annotated, and the 3D $(x,y,z)$ joints are obtained by multi‑view triangulation of a fine‑tuned Mask R‑CNN detector, not from monocular 3D pose.[^1_1]

## 2D COCO‑format keypoints

- The dataset contains **2D human joint annotations in COCO format for about 1% of all frames**, chosen as keyframes that span diverse poses and viewpoints.[^1_1]
- These 2D keypoints were **manually labeled on Amazon Mechanical Turk**, where workers annotated **12 body joints plus 5 face keypoints** (17 in total) per person and supplied a confidence score (1–3). Annotations were then visually checked and corrected if needed.[^1_1]
- In other words, the canonical 17‑joint COCO skeletons in IKEA ASM are **hand‑annotated**, not the output of any off‑the‑shelf 2D pose estimator such as OpenPose, HRNet, or ViTPose.[^1_1]

So if you are using the official 2D annotations from the dataset, you are using **manual COCO‑style labels**, not OpenPose/HRNet/ViTPose outputs.

## Pose estimators used only as baselines

The paper does run several pose estimators on IKEA ASM, but only for **benchmarking**, not for label generation:[^1_1]

- **2D baselines:** OpenPose, Mask R‑CNN (ResNet‑50‑FPN keypoint head), and Spatio‑Temporal Affinity Fields (STAF), all pre‑trained on COCO/MPII and then fine‑tuned on IKEA ASM’s manual 2D labels.[^1_1]
- **3D baselines (monocular):** HMMR, VideoPose3D, and VIBE, evaluated against the dataset’s 3D pseudo‑ground‑truth.[^1_1]

These models’ predictions are **not** the dataset’s “official” skeletons; they’re just benchmark results.

## How 3D $(x,y,z)$ joints are obtained

The dataset includes three **calibrated** Kinect V2 RGB cameras (front, side, top) plus one depth stream.  For 3D human pose, the authors construct **pseudo‑ground‑truth 3D joints** via a multi‑view pipeline:[^1_1]

1. **Fine‑tune a 2D joint detector**
    - They take **Mask R‑CNN with a keypoint head** (ResNet‑50‑FPN) and fine‑tune it on the manually labeled 2D keypoints.[^1_1]
2. **Run it on all three RGB views**
    - For each frame, they run this Mask R‑CNN detector on the **three synchronized, calibrated camera views** to get 2D joint locations in each view.[^1_1]
3. **Triangulate to 3D**
    - Using the known camera intrinsics and extrinsics, they **triangulate corresponding 2D joints across the three views** to obtain 3D joint coordinates $(x,y,z)$.[^1_1]
4. **Filter using reprojection error**
    - As a quality check, each 3D point is **backprojected** into the image planes. If the reprojected 2D point is **more than 30 pixels away** from the most confident manual 2D annotation, that 3D joint is discarded.[^1_1]
    - After this filtering, the average reprojection error is about **7.1 pixels on the train set** and **9.1 pixels on the test set**, over the accepted joints.[^1_1]

Because of this automatic pipeline and filtering, the authors refer to these 3D labels as **“pseudo‑ground‑truth 3D annotations”** rather than fully manual 3D ground truth.[^1_1]

## Monocular vs multi‑view in IKEA ASM

- **Ground‑truth 3D (what you get from the dataset)**
    - Comes from **multi‑view triangulation** of Mask R‑CNN 2D joints across the three calibrated cameras, with reprojection filtering.[^1_1]
    - It does **not** come from monocular video alone.
- **Monocular 3D pose in the paper**
    - Models like HMMR, VideoPose3D, and VIBE are evaluated **as monocular 3D baselines** against that multi‑view 3D pseudo‑GT. Their predictions are **not** used as labels in the dataset.[^1_1]

So if you load the official 3D joint annotations (x, y, z) from IKEA ASM, you are using **multi‑view triangulated 3D joints derived from a fine‑tuned Mask R‑CNN 2D detector plus manual 2D labels**, not monocular 3D estimates and not OpenPose/HRNet/ViTPose outputs.[^1_1]

# COCO 17 keypoint skeleton definition. What are the exact 17 joint names in order,

their indices 0-16, and the standard skeleton connections/limbs?
Why does COCO use 17 keypoints and not more for human pose estimation?
What body parts are covered and what is excluded?

Here’s the standard COCO 17‑keypoint definition and how the skeleton is wired.

## 17 keypoints: names and indices

The canonical COCO ordering and indices 0–16 are:

0. Nose
1. Left eye
2. Right eye
3. Left ear
4. Right ear
5. Left shoulder
6. Right shoulder
7. Left elbow
8. Right elbow
9. Left wrist
10. Right wrist
11. Left hip
12. Right hip
13. Left knee
14. Right knee
15. Left ankle
16. Right ankle[^2_7][^2_9]

You’ll see this exact order in many pose libraries (e.g., YOLO pose, Detectron, etc.) because they follow the COCO keypoints spec.[^2_7]

## Standard skeleton connections (limbs)

COCO also defines a default “skeleton” as an ordered list of edges between keypoints (used for visualization and sometimes loss definitions). A common connectivity used with COCO keypoints is:

- Nose – Left eye
- Nose – Right eye
- Left eye – Left ear
- Right eye – Right ear
- Nose – Left shoulder
- Nose – Right shoulder
- Left shoulder – Left elbow
- Left elbow – Left wrist
- Right shoulder – Right elbow
- Right elbow – Right wrist
- Left shoulder – Left hip
- Right shoulder – Right hip
- Left hip – Left knee
- Left knee – Left ankle
- Right hip – Right knee
- Right knee – Right ankle

In index form (using 1‑based indices as in the original JSON skeleton lists, then subtracting 1 for 0‑based code):

- (0,1), (0,2), (1,3), (2,4), (0,5), (0,6), (5,7), (7,9), (6,8), (8,10), (5,11), (6,12), (11,13), (13,15), (12,14), (14,16)

Exact edge ordering can vary slightly by implementation, but these are the standard COCO body connections.[^2_1]

## Why COCO uses 17 keypoints (not more)

COCO’s 17‑point schema is a **design compromise** between annotation cost and pose descriptiveness:

- It covers the **major anatomical joints** needed to describe full‑body pose for common activities: face orientation (nose, eyes, ears), upper body (shoulders, elbows, wrists), and lower body (hips, knees, ankles).[^2_2][^2_3]
- Annotating many fine‑grained points (e.g., fingers, toes, detailed facial landmarks) across 200k+ humans would be **expensive and inconsistent**, especially for small or occluded parts.[^2_10][^2_2]
- At the time COCO Keypoints was introduced, the main use cases were **generic human pose estimation and action recognition**, not fine‑grained hand or face tracking, so 17 “macro” joints were sufficient.[^2_11][^2_2]

Later datasets such as COCO‑WholeBody extend COCO with **133 keypoints** (17 body, 6 feet, 68 face, 42 hands) when richer articulation is needed, but plain COCO sticks to 17 for compatibility and efficiency.[^2_10]

## What is covered vs excluded

**Covered (explicit joints):**

- Head/face: nose, left/right eye, left/right ear → gives rough head orientation and location.[^2_9]
- Upper body: left/right shoulder, elbow, wrist → full 3‑segment arms and shoulder line.[^2_9]
- Lower body: left/right hip, knee, ankle → full 3‑segment legs and pelvis line.[^2_9]

**Excluded (no separate keypoints):**

- Detailed **face** structure (mouth corners, chin, eyebrows, more eyes/ears landmarks).
- **Hands** and fingers (no per‑finger joints, no palms).
- **Feet** details (no toes, heel, mid‑foot; just ankle).
- Torso center, neck, spine, or other intermediate joints (neck is sometimes inferred virtually as midpoint between shoulders, pelvis as midpoint between hips, but not annotated explicitly).[^2_2][^2_10]

In practice, this means:

- COCO is excellent for **full‑body coarse pose** and action recognition.
- It is insufficient for tasks demanding detailed **hand pose**, **facial expression**, or precise **foot placement**, which is why extended schemas like COCO‑WholeBody or dedicated hand/face datasets are used in those domains.[^2_10][^2_2]

# Root-relative pose normalization for skeleton-based action recognition.

How is the 51D pose vector normalized? What is the reference joint (hip midpoint)?
Why normalize relative to hip rather than absolute coordinates?
What scale normalization is applied to make pose invariant to camera distance?

Root-relative pose normalization is a foundational preprocessing step in skeleton-based action recognition, converting raw world- or camera-space 3D joint coordinates into a canonical, person-centred representation. Here is a precise breakdown of every component.

## The 51D pose vector

With COCO's 17 joints, each having an $(x, y, z)$ coordinate, a single pose frame is represented as a **51-dimensional vector** obtained by flattening the $(17 \times 3)$ joint matrix:[^3_1]

$$
\mathbf{p} = [x_0, y_0, z_0,\; x_1, y_1, z_1,\; \ldots,\; x_{16}, y_{16}, z_{16}] \in \mathbb{R}^{51}
$$

Before flattening, **root-relative and scale normalization** are applied so the 51 values are invariant to global position and camera distance.[^3_2][^3_1]

## The reference (root) joint: hip midpoint

The **hip center (pelvis midpoint)** is computed as the average of the left hip (index 11) and right hip (index 12):[^3_2][^3_3]

$$
\mathbf{r} = \frac{1}{2}\left(J_{11} + J_{12}\right)
$$

This virtual joint is then subtracted from all 17 joints:

$$
\tilde{J}_i = J_i - \mathbf{r}, \quad i = 0, \ldots, 16
$$

This is called **root-relative normalization** (or root subtraction).  Note that this makes the hip-center joint itself become $\mathbf{0}$, anchoring the skeleton at the origin.[^3_1][^3_3]

## Why the hip, not another joint?

The hip midpoint is the de facto root for several biomechanical and practical reasons:[^3_2][^3_4]

- **Centre of mass proxy.** The pelvis is the anatomical centre of the human body; all major limb chains (arms, legs, head) radiate from it, so it is the most stable reference for encoding relative joint positions.
- **Low noise.** The hips are rarely occluded and their depth estimates are typically more stable than distal joints (wrists, ankles), making the root signal reliable.
- **Disentangles global translation from local pose.** Absolute position (where the person stands in the room) is irrelevant to recognizing an action; root-subtraction strips it away so the model focuses on shape rather than location.[^3_1][^3_3]
- **Standard convention.** Human3.6M, NTU RGB+D, IKEA ASM, and most benchmark datasets compute MPJPE after aligning the hip/pelvis centroid of the predicted and ground-truth skeletons, making hip-relative coordinates the lingua franca for evaluation.[^3_3][^3_5]

The IKEA ASM paper explicitly computes MPJPE **after aligning the centroids of the 3D points**, following the same convention as Human3.6M.[^3_5]

## Scale normalization: camera-distance invariance

Raw 3D joints from monocular depth or triangulation vary in magnitude with camera distance. Multiple scale normalization strategies are used:[^3_6][^3_7][^3_4]

### Torso / reference bone normalization

The most common approach is to **divide all root-relative joints by a reference bone length** — typically the torso diagonal (e.g., distance from pelvis to neck, or left-shoulder to right-hip):[^3_2][^3_3][^3_4]

$$
\hat{J}_i = \frac{\tilde{J}_i}{\| J_{\text{shoulder}} - J_{\text{hip}} \|}
$$

This makes the representation invariant to both body size and camera distance. The per-dataset reference skeleton variant: normalize each bone length to match a **canonical reference skeleton** chosen from the training set, preserving joint angles but equalizing bone lengths.[^3_3][^3_4]

### Pixel / image-space normalization

For 2D keypoints (or 2D-projected 3D), dividing $x$ by image width and $y$ by image height maps them into $[0,1]$, removing the dependency on image resolution and approximate camera distance.[^3_8]

### Depth normalization for monocular models

When root depth $d$ is estimated, it is normalized by the camera focal length $f_c$ as $\tilde{d} = d / f_c$, and joint offsets are then scaled by $1/\tilde{d}$ to undo the perspective shrinkage that makes a person's joints appear closer together at greater distance.[^3_9]

## What is retained vs removed

| Property | After normalization | Rationale |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Global translation $(x,y,z)$ of body | **Removed** (root subtraction) | Action is position-independent [^3_1][^3_3] |
| Camera distance / body scale | **Removed** (bone-length scaling) | Same action looks different at 1m vs 5m [^3_6][^3_4] |
| Relative joint positions | **Preserved** | Encodes actual pose/shape [^3_2] |
| Joint angles and limb directions | **Preserved** | Core motion descriptor [^3_6] |
| Global body orientation (viewpoint) | **Optionally removed** (view normalization) | Some methods further rotate to a canonical front-facing frame [^3_10][^3_11] |

The result is a compact 51D vector per frame where each value encodes **how far a joint is from the pelvis, in units of body size**, making it invariant to who is performing the action, where they stand, and how far the camera is.[^3_1][^3_2][^3_3]

# Why ResNet-50 for assembly action recognition instead of Vision Transformer (ViT)

or EfficientNet? What is the parameter count, FLOPs, and inference speed of
ResNet-50 vs alternatives for video frame understanding with small datasets
(under 10,000 training samples)? When does CNN outperform ViT on small datasets?

Using ResNet‑50 as a 2D backbone for assembly/assembly‑like action recognition on <10k samples is usually a better default than ViT‑B/16 and often more practical than EfficientNet, because it has strong inductive bias for local patterns, moderate capacity, abundant pretrained checkpoints, and substantially lower data and compute requirements than ViT while still being easier to optimize than very small CNNs for complex scenes.[^1_1][^1_2][^1_3]

## Backbone size, FLOPs, and per‑image speed

For standard 224×224 inputs (single image / frame):


| Model | Params (M) | FLOPs (G) @224² | Example single‑image latency (GPU) |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| ResNet‑50 | ≈25–25.6 M | ≈4.1 G | ≈23 ms (T4, batch=1) |
| EfficientNet‑B0 | 5.3 M | 0.39 G | ≈10–11 ms (T4, batch=1) |
| ViT‑B/16 | 86.6 M | 17.6 G | ≈45 ms (T4, batch=1) |

- A detailed FLOP/parameter analysis of torchvision’s ResNet‑50 reports about 25.6M parameters and 4.145 GFLOPs at 224×224.[^1_4]
- A recent benchmark of backbones under a common pipeline gives very similar numbers for ResNet‑50 (≈25M params, 4.09 GFLOPs) and EfficientNet‑B0 (5.3M params, 0.39 GFLOPs), with measured per‑image latencies of ≈23 ms vs ≈11 ms on a Tesla T4 (batch=1).[^1_5]
- EfficientNet‑B0’s original description reports 5.3M parameters and 0.39 GFLOPs at 224×224, confirming its much lower compute than classic CNNs.[^1_6][^1_7]
- State‑of‑the‑art ViT surveys list ViT‑B/16 at ≈86.6M parameters and 17.6 GFLOPs for 224×224 inputs, about 3–4× the parameters and ~4× FLOPs of ResNet‑50.[^1_8]

On a V100 under a unified implementation, one comparison reports throughput of roughly 2,694 img/s for EfficientNet‑B0, 1,226 img/s for ResNet‑50, and 292 img/s for ViT‑B/16‑like DeiT‑B (same backbone shape), again showing a ~4× gap in speed between ResNet‑50 and ViT at fixed resolution.[^1_3]

For video: FLOPs and latency scale roughly linearly with the number of frames you process per clip. For example, for a 32‑frame clip at 224×224, you’re in the ballpark of ~130 GFLOPs for ResNet‑50, ~12–13 GFLOPs for EfficientNet‑B0, and ~560 GFLOPs for ViT‑B/16, so ViT can easily become the dominant cost in a 2D‑backbone‑plus‑temporal‑head pipeline.[^1_7][^1_9][^1_6][^1_8]

## Why ResNet‑50 is a solid default on <10k samples

For small labeled datasets (especially <10k images or clips) three things matter a lot: inductive bias, optimization ease, and available pretraining. ResNet‑50 is strong on all three.

- **Strong local inductive bias and translation equivariance.** Convolutions bake in the assumptions that nearby pixels are related and features are translation‑equivariant, so a ResNet backbone “knows” to look for local edges, corners, hands, tools, etc. without needing many examples.[^1_10][^1_1]
- **Sample efficiency in small‑data regime.** Multiple works show that plain ViTs trained from scratch underperform comparable CNNs on small datasets like CIFAR‑100 and low‑resolution medical datasets, unless they are given additional locality or pretraining; the lack of convolutional inductive bias makes ViTs much more data‑hungry.[^1_2][^1_1][^1_10]
- **Optimization robustness.** ResNets have been tuned for a decade; with standard augmentation and ImageNet initialization, fine‑tuning on a few thousand images generally converges reliably, whereas ViTs need carefully chosen optimizers, schedules, and strong augmentation just to avoid underfitting or divergence.[^1_3]
- **Good capacity for complex scenes.** ResNet‑50’s ~25M parameters and relatively deep hierarchy give more representational capacity than lightweight models (e.g., EfficientNet‑B0, MobileNet), which can matter in cluttered assembly scenes with hands, tools, parts, and background clutter.[^1_5]

For assembly/action recognition, you typically extract 2D features per frame and then feed them into a temporal module (1D TCN, LSTM, transformer, or 3D head). ResNet‑50 is widely supported in video libraries and has abundant pretrained weights (ImageNet and sometimes Kinetics), making it straightforward to plug into this kind of pipeline and fine‑tune on <10k clips without catastrophic overfitting.[^1_3]

## Where EfficientNet fits

EfficientNet‑B0 is much smaller and faster than ResNet‑50 while still achieving strong accuracy on many image classification tasks.[^1_6][^1_5]

- One comprehensive comparison across backbones finds EfficientNet‑B0 has the highest “accuracy per parameter” and “accuracy per GFLOP” among the tested models, vastly better parameter efficiency than both ResNet‑50 and ViT‑Base.[^1_11][^1_5]
- On the same GPU, EfficientNet‑B0 is roughly 2–3× faster than ResNet‑50 at 224×224, and an order of magnitude faster than ViT‑B/16‑style models.[^1_5][^1_3]

However, for assembly action recognition specifically:

- The tiny parameter budget and aggressive depth/width scaling can limit representational capacity for very detailed pose/hand‑object interactions, especially if you are not using carefully tuned augmentations and regularization that EfficientNets often rely on.[^1_6]
- Pretrained EfficientNet backbones are less standardized in video frameworks than ResNet‑50, so you may lose ecosystem support (off‑the‑shelf checkpoints and heads).

So EfficientNet‑B0 is attractive when latency, memory, or energy are primary constraints (edge deployment, embedded devices), and you’re willing to trade some feature richness for efficiency. For a research setting with <10k clips and GPU inference, ResNet‑50 is usually the more forgiving backbone.

## Why plain ViT is usually a bad idea on <10k samples

The core issue: ViTs replace convolution with global self‑attention over patch tokens and drop most spatial inductive bias. This helps at scale but hurts when labeled data are scarce.

- A study on enhancing ViT performance on small datasets explicitly notes that “ViTs achieve remarkable performance on large datasets, but tend to perform worse than CNNs when trained from scratch on smaller datasets, possibly due to a lack of local inductive bias,” and shows that adding local modules narrows the gap.[^1_1]
- The “Bridging the Gap between Vision Transformers and CNNs” work reports that standard ViT architectures, which are competitive with CNNs on ImageNet‑1K when strongly tuned, still “fail to compete with standard CNNs” when trained from scratch on smaller datasets like CIFAR‑100, and closes the gap only by explicitly adding convolutional/local constraints.[^1_2]
- Another analysis highlights that ViTs underperform CNNs on small, low‑resolution datasets (e.g., DermaMNIST, Tiny ImageNet subsets) and are more computationally demanding at inference, again attributing this to missing locality and the need for more data.[^1_12]

The DeiT paper shows that with very strong augmentation and knowledge distillation from a CNN teacher, ViT‑B/16‑like models can be trained on ImageNet‑1K alone and become competitive with EfficientNet‑B family, but this assumes you *already* have 1.2M labeled images and heavy training. That regime is very different from a <10k‑sample assembly dataset.[^1_3]

Empirically, unless you have:

- large‑scale supervised pretraining (e.g., ImageNet‑21k, JFT‑like) or
- powerful self‑supervised pretraining on related video/image data (e.g., MAE‑style pretraining, which has been shown to make ViTs less data‑hungry),[^1_13]

a plain ViT‑B/16 backbone fine‑tuned directly on <10k assembly clips is likely to overfit, underperform a well‑tuned ResNet‑50, and cost you ~4× compute per frame.

## When CNNs outperform ViTs on small datasets

From the recent literature, CNNs typically beat or match ViTs in these conditions:

- **Training from scratch with limited labels (<100k, especially <10k).** On small datasets like CIFAR‑100 or low‑resolution medical datasets, standard ViTs trained from scratch underperform ResNets, while CNNs remain strong; only ViTs with added convolutional tokenization or local modules recover CNN‑level accuracy.[^1_12][^1_1][^1_2]
- **Low‑resolution images (e.g., 32–128 px) or small objects.** CNNs’ local receptive fields and hierarchical pooling are well suited to low‑resolution signals, while ViTs’ fixed patch size and lack of inherent multi‑scale structure make them brittle unless architecturally modified.[^1_12]
- **Limited compute and need for fast inference.** Lightweight or mid‑range CNNs (EfficientNet‑B0, MobileNet, ResNet‑18/50) often deliver better accuracy‑latency trade‑offs than ViTs for small/medium models; one comparative study finds CNNs have faster average training time and much higher “accuracy per parameter” than ViT‑Base.[^1_11]

ViTs start to shine (and often surpass CNNs) when:

- You have **millions of labeled images** or can leverage strong self‑supervised pretraining, which lets the transformer learn its own inductive biases instead of relying on built‑in convolutional ones.[^1_10][^1_13][^1_3]
- You operate on **high‑resolution images** or tasks that really benefit from long‑range interactions, like large‑scale detection and segmentation, and you can afford the extra compute.[^1_8][^1_10]
- You adopt **hybrid or locality‑enhanced ViTs** (e.g., convolutional tokenization, local attention, added local modules) that explicitly reintroduce inductive bias for small datasets.[^1_14][^1_1][^1_2]


## Practical guidance for assembly action recognition (<10k samples)

For your specific use case—per‑frame video understanding for assembly/assembly‑like actions with <10k labeled clips—reasonable defaults are:

- **Backbone choice:**
    - Use **ResNet‑50 + temporal head** (TCN/1D conv/LSTM/temporal transformer) as the main baseline; it balances capacity, inductive bias, and robustness on small data.[^1_5][^1_3]
    - Consider **EfficientNet‑B0** when you have strict latency or memory limits (embedded/edge deployment), accepting a potential hit in representation capacity for fine‑grained hand‑object interactions.[^1_6][^1_5]
    - Avoid **plain ViT‑B/16** unless you:
        - start from a very strong pretrained checkpoint (e.g., MAE/CLIP/large‑scale video) and
        - are willing to pay ~4× FLOPs and latency per frame and tune heavy augmentation/distillation.[^1_13][^1_8][^1_3]
- **When to consider ViT‑style models anyway:**
    - If you can pretrain on a large pool of unlabeled assembly or egocentric videos using self‑supervised ViT methods (MAE‑like), ViT backbones may then become competitive or superior even on small labeled subsets.[^1_13]
    - If you adopt *locality‑enhanced* ViTs (e.g., convolutional tokenization, local information enhancer modules) specifically designed to improve performance on small datasets, which several papers show can match CNNs while keeping transformer benefits.[^1_14][^1_1][^1_2]

In short: for <10k labeled assembly clips with standard 2D‑backbone‑plus‑temporal‑head training, ResNet‑50 is usually the safest and most balanced choice; EfficientNet‑B0 is the efficiency‑optimized alternative; ViT becomes attractive only once you bring in strong pretraining or architecture tweaks that compensate for its data hunger.

# FiLM Feature-wise Linear Modulation applied to visual reasoning.

Perez et al. AAAI 2018. What is the exact MLP architecture used to predict
gamma and beta parameters? What layer of the visual encoder does FiLM condition?
Why condition on the last convolutional layer vs earlier layers?
What is the computational overhead of FiLM conditioning?

I now have the full paper content from the primary source. Let me compose a precise, well-cited answer.

***

FiLM (Feature-wise Linear Modulation) by Perez et al. conditions a CNN's intermediate feature maps using a simple per-channel affine transform $\text{FiLM}(\mathbf{F}_{i,c} \mid \gamma_{i,c}, \beta_{i,c}) = \gamma_{i,c} \mathbf{F}_{i,c} + \beta_{i,c}$, where $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are predicted dynamically from language input.[^2_1]

## The FiLM Generator Architecture

The FiLM generator — the network that *predicts* $\gamma$ and $\beta$ — is **not** an MLP operating independently. It is a **GRU followed by a single linear (affine) projection**:[^2_1]

1. **Input embedding:** Each word in the question is mapped to a **200-dimensional learned word embedding**.
2. **GRU encoder:** A single GRU with **4096 hidden units** reads the word embeddings sequentially.
3. **Linear readout:** The **final GRU hidden state** (a 4096-dim vector) is projected via a separate **linear (affine) layer per ResBlock** to produce the $(\boldsymbol{\gamma}^n, \boldsymbol{\beta}^n)$ pair for each $n$-th residual block.

For the default 4-ResBlock model with 128 feature maps per block, each linear projection outputs $2 \times 128 = 256$ values (128 γs and 128 βs), so the full output of the FiLM generator across all ResBlocks is $4 \times 256 = 1024$ scalar conditioning parameters.[^2_1]

The **answer classifier** — a separate component — uses a two-layer MLP with 1024 hidden units, fed from a 1×1 conv (→ 512 maps) followed by global max-pooling. This classifier MLP is **not** the FiLM generator.[^2_1]

## Which Layers Are Conditioned

FiLM does **not** target only the last convolutional layer. It conditions **every residual block in the FiLM-ed visual pipeline** — all 4 ResBlocks by default. Each FiLM-ed ResBlock has the structure:[^2_1]

- Input → **1×1 conv** → **3×3 conv** → **Batch Norm (with BN affine params turned off)** → **FiLM layer** → ReLU → residual add

The key detail is that BN's own learnable affine parameters are disabled immediately before each FiLM layer, so FiLM *replaces* the BN affine transformation rather than stacking on top of it. This makes FiLM a strict generalization of Conditional Batch Normalization.[^2_1]

When using a pretrained backbone (ResNet-101), the image features are extracted from **conv4** (a fixed 14×14 spatial feature map), and FiLM conditions the *separate* FiLM-ed ResBlocks stacked on top — not the ResNet-101's internal layers.[^2_1]

## Why Condition at Each Layer, Not Just the Last

The paper's t-SNE analysis of FiLM parameters directly answers this. Early FiLM layers cluster around **low-level question sub-functions** (e.g., distinguishing `query_color` from `query_shape`), while later FiLM layers cluster by **high-level reasoning operations** (e.g., grouping `equal_shape`, `equal_size`, `equal_material` together). This mirrors the layer-wise hierarchy of a CNN, where lower layers encode texture/color and upper layers encode semantics.[^2_1]

- **Early conditioning** lets the network suppress irrelevant low-level features (e.g., ignore all non-red pixels when the question asks about red objects) by using $\gamma < 0$ or $\gamma \approx 0$ to zero/negate channels before a ReLU.[^2_1]
- **Late conditioning** guides high-level relational reasoning (e.g., counting, comparing sizes) in deeper feature spaces that encode object-level semantics.[^2_1]
- **Ablation result:** Even a model with a **single FiLM layer** placed anywhere in the network does not deviate dramatically from peak accuracy, suggesting even one well-placed modulation can inject enough question signal — but multi-layer conditioning is significantly more interpretable and learns cleaner compositional structure.[^2_1]

The de Vries et al. (2017) concurrent work conditioned *only* early visual processing via language, while FiLM's key insight is that **repetitive, hierarchical conditioning** throughout the network lets language guide progressively abstract visual computations.[^2_2][^2_1]

## Computational Overhead of FiLM

The overhead is **extremely low** — this is one of FiLM's primary advantages:[^2_1]


| Cost component | Details |
| :-- | :-- |
| FiLM forward pass | $2 \times C \times H \times W$ multiply-adds per FiLM layer (one scale + one shift per activation) |
| Scales with resolution? | **No** — $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are scalars per channel, not per spatial location; the affine transform is broadcast over spatial dims |
| Scales with image size? | **No** — FiLM generator (GRU + linear) runs on question only; its cost is independent of $H \times W$ |
| Extra parameters | Only **2 × C** new parameters per FiLM layer (C gammas and C betas) — e.g., 256 scalars for a 128-channel block |
| GRU generator cost | Fixed at O(sequence length × 4096²) regardless of image size |

For a 128-channel, 14×14 feature map, each FiLM layer adds only $2 \times 128 \times 14 \times 14 = 50{,}176$ multiply-adds — negligible compared to the $\sim$20M+ multiply-adds in a single 3×3 conv layer at the same resolution. The paper explicitly states: *"FiLM has a computational cost that does not scale with the image resolution"*, making it practical for high-resolution video frame conditioning as well.[^2_1]

The generator's GRU (4096 hidden units) is the dominant cost on the language side, but it processes the question *once* per inference and its output is reused across all FiLM layers via separate lightweight linear projections.[^2_1]

## Summary of Architecture

```
Question tokens
     │
[200-d word embeddings]
     │
[GRU, 4096 hidden units]
     │
 final hidden state (4096-d)
     │
 ┌───┴──────────────┐
 │ Linear (×N_blocks)│  → (γⁿ, βⁿ) ∈ ℝ^(2×128), per ResBlock n
 └───────────────────┘

Image (224×224) → [Conv backbone / ResNet-101 conv4]
     │
 [FiLM-ed ResBlock 1]: 1×1 conv → 3×3 conv → BN (no affine) → FiLM(γ¹,β¹) → ReLU
 [FiLM-ed ResBlock 2]: same, FiLM(γ²,β²)
 [FiLM-ed ResBlock 3]: same, FiLM(γ³,β³)
 [FiLM-ed ResBlock 4]: same, FiLM(γ⁴,β⁴)
     │
 [1×1 conv → 512 maps → Global Max Pool]
     │
 [2-layer MLP, 1024 hidden → softmax over answers]
```

This architecture is exactly as described in Section 2.2 of the paper. For your assembly action recognition work, this maps cleanly to: GRU over a natural-language instruction or action label → FiLM conditioning of each ResBlock in your per-frame feature extractor → temporal head — giving you dynamic, query-dependent feature suppression/amplification with negligible inference overhead.[^2_2][^2_1]

# ResNet-50 architecture details: exact channel dimensions at each stage C1 C2 C3 C4 C5.

What is the spatial resolution and channel count after each residual block stage?
Why does C5 have 2048 channels? What is the bottleneck design in ResNet-50?
Tensor shape at each stage for input 224x224.

ResNet-50 is organized into a **stem (C1)** and **four residual stages (C2–C5)**, each doubling channel width while halving spatial resolution, for a total stride of 32 between the raw input and C5. For a 224×224×3 input, every stage outputs a precisely predictable tensor shape.[^3_1][^3_2]

## Full Stage-by-Stage Tensor Flow

| Stage | Layer(s) | Kernel / Op | Stride | Blocks | Output Tensor (N, C, H, W) | Inner bottleneck width |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Stem** | conv1 | 7×7, 64 | 2 | — | N × 64 × 112 × 112 | — |
| **C1** | max pool | 3×3 | 2 | — | N × 64 × 56 × 56 | — |
| **C2** | conv2_x | bottleneck × 3 | 1 | 3 | **N × 256 × 56 × 56** | 64 |
| **C3** | conv3_x | bottleneck × 4 | 2¹ | 4 | **N × 512 × 28 × 28** | 128 |
| **C4** | conv4_x | bottleneck × 6 | 2¹ | 6 | **N × 1024 × 14 × 14** | 256 |
| **C5** | conv5_x | bottleneck × 3 | 2¹ | 3 | **N × 2048 × 7 × 7** | 512 |
| **Head** | global avg pool → FC | — | — | — | N × 2048 → N × 1000 | — |

¹ Stride 2 applied only in the **first** bottleneck of that stage (via the 3×3 conv or the projection shortcut).[^3_2][^3_3][^3_1]

The cumulative spatial downsampling is: 224 / 2 (stem conv) / 2 (max pool) / 2 (C3) / 2 (C4) / 2 (C5) = **7**, giving the well-known 7×7 at C5.[^3_3]

***

## The Bottleneck Block Design

Every block in C2–C5 follows the **1×1 → 3×3 → 1×1 bottleneck pattern** from He et al.:[^3_4][^3_5]

```
Input (e.g. 256-ch at C2)
   │
 [1×1 conv, width W]          ← compress: 256 → 64
   │
 [3×3 conv, width W]          ← spatial mixing at reduced cost: 64 → 64
   │
 [1×1 conv, width W×4]        ← expand: 64 → 256
   │ 
 [+ skip connection (identity or 1×1 proj)]
   │
 Output (256-ch)
```

The **expansion ratio is exactly 4×**: the first 1×1 compresses to the "bottleneck width" W, and the last 1×1 expands back to W×4. The inner widths per stage are 64, 128, 256, 512 — each multiplied by 4 gives the stage's output channel count: 256, 512, 1024, 2048.[^3_6][^3_5]

The key principle is that the **3×3 convolution runs at reduced channel width** (e.g., 64 instead of 256), which sharply cuts the dominant spatial convolution cost while the cheap 1×1s handle channel mixing. Without the bottleneck, a 3×3 conv on 256 channels costs $3^2 \times 256^2 = 589{,}824$ multiply-adds per spatial location; with the bottleneck the same step costs $3^2 \times 64^2 = 36{,}864$ — a **16× reduction** for that layer alone.[^3_7][^3_4]

The **first block of every stage (C3–C5)** uses a **1×1 projection shortcut** (also stride 2) to match the changing spatial and channel dimensions before adding the residual.[^3_2][^3_4]

***

## Why C5 Has 2048 Channels

This follows directly from the **doubling schedule and fixed 4× expansion ratio**:[^3_6][^3_2]

$$
\text{C5 output channels} = 512_{\text{inner}} \times 4_{\text{expansion}} = 2048
$$

The channel-doubling schedule (64 → 128 → 256 → 512 inner widths across C2–C5) is a deliberate design choice in He et al. that keeps the **computational cost roughly constant per stage**: halving the spatial size ($H \times W$) while doubling channels means total activations per stage stay roughly stable in memory and FLOPs. Stopping at 2048 at C5 already gives the network rich discriminative capacity for downstream tasks (classification, detection, segmentation), and going wider would push memory and FLOPs beyond practical limits without commensurate accuracy gain.[^3_2]

***

## Why C4 and C5 Are the Most Important for Downstream Tasks

In object detection and action recognition, feature extractors are typically attached at C4 (14×14×1024) or C5 (7×7×2048):[^3_1]

- **C4** preserves more spatial detail (14×14 vs 7×7) and is the preferred backbone output in Faster R-CNN and many temporal models, traded against C5's richer semantics.[^3_1]
- **C5** (7×7) is the natural classification feature and the basis for FPN's top-down pathway in multi-scale detection.[^3_8]
- For FiLM-style conditioning on ResNet-50, the FiLM-ed ResBlocks stacked after a frozen conv4 backbone operate on **14×14×1024** feature maps — which is exactly why FiLM's spatial-broadcast affine transform is so cheap at that resolution.[^3_9]

For your IKEA ASM assembly pipeline, this means your 2D frame features at C4 are **14×14×1024** per frame (at 224×224 input), or **28×28×1024** at 448×448 input — the exact tensor you feed into a temporal head or FiLM-conditioning stack.[^3_3][^3_1]
